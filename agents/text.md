# Writing a page's text

This is for whoever writes or edits the English text of a page.

A page has a figure the reader can play with and a short text beside it. The reader is a learner at the story's
level, of any age, often on a phone. The text is there to help them understand one idea.

## What matters, in order

When two things pull against each other, the one higher in this list wins. Then you meet the lower one as well as
you can.

1. **The maths is right.** Every number, every claim and every step.
2. **The reader understands the idea, and why it works.** A text that lists steps and explains nothing has failed.
3. **It sounds like a person.** A tutor explaining something to one learner, in ordinary English.
4. **The terms and the conventions of the book hold.** They are in the last section of this file.
5. **It is short.** Nothing is there that the reader could do without.

## The voice

Write the way a good tutor talks to one learner who is sitting next to them. The tutor is calm and clear. They use
ordinary words, they say why things are so, and they do not show off. They are not chatty either: no filler, no
jokes, no "Let's dive in".

Here is the test. Read your sentence out loud. Would a tutor say it, in exactly those words, to a learner? If it
sounds translated, squeezed, or like a manual, write it again.

Know when to stop, too. Rewrite a sentence when it is unnatural, unclear, misleading, or harder to follow than it
needs to be. Once it is none of those, leave it alone. Being able to think of another wording is not a reason to
change it.

Being grammatically correct is not enough. Plenty of correct sentences are ones no native speaker would ever say.
"The sum is how many altogether" is correct, and nobody talks like that. A person says "The sum tells you how many
there are altogether." If nobody would say it, do not write it. That goes for titles and for the words on the
figure too.

A few habits help:

- Talk to the reader as "you" when you describe what they do or how they think it through.
- Say who does what. "You find the number by counting from the nearest end" is easier to follow than "The number is
  found by counting from the nearest end." A passive that people really say is fine: "4 apples were eaten", "how
  many are left", "this is called the difference".
- Give each sentence one main idea. Join two clauses when one explains the other, with "because", "so", "if" or
  "when".
- Use the everyday word: "the mark for 4", "this no longer works", "to fix the line".

Each row below is a sentence this book once had, and the way a person would say it.

| Squeezed or stiff | The way a person says it |
|---|---|
| Gaps of different lengths break that. | If the gaps have different lengths, this no longer works. |
| A blue counter in the second frame while the first frame has an empty cell hides the steps. | If you drop a blue counter in the second frame while the first frame still has an empty cell, no steps appear. |
| With every seed sown, the sum and the two steps show under the tray. | When you have sown every seed, the sum and the two steps appear under the tray. |
| Under the tray stand three places. | There are three places under the tray. |
| The figure fills every counter up to the marker. | Every counter up to the marker is filled in. |
| You say "plus" for the sign +. | You say "plus" for the + sign. |

These rows show the kind of thing to listen for. They are not a list of banned words. The first row is not wrong
because of any one word in it. It is wrong because nobody would say it.

### Slips this book keeps making

A reviewer looks for these, and a writer who knows them makes fewer. Each one is a habit, and your ear decides each
case.

- A thing "stands", "sits", "waits" or "lies" somewhere, when a person would say it "is" there.
- "The steps show" or "the bracket hides", with nothing after the verb. People say "appear" and "disappear".
- A sentence that opens on a loose phrase: "With the middle marked, ...", "Paired, the rows ...".
- Chains of "of": "the mark of 4", "the number of the stone". People say "the mark for 4", "the stone's number".
- The name after the thing: "the sign +", "the readout In words". People say "the + sign", "the "In words" readout".
- An idea doing what a person does: "Counting reaches 7", "The pairing shows". Say who counts and who sees.
- An active sentence forced where the passive is natural: "Someone ate 4 apples", "The figure sets out the counters".
- A sentence about the page or the screen: "the page opens with the point on 14". Say "the point starts on 14".
- A run of readings: "the number sold is 3, the number left is 7, and the number on the shelf is 10".

## What a text does

Most texts do three things, in this order.

1. **Teach the idea.** One or two short paragraphs in plain words: what the idea is, and why it works. Leave the
   buttons and the readouts out of this part. The first paragraph is what the reader sees on the folded card under
   the figure, so it has to make sense alone.
2. **Work one example.** Take one case and go through it in numbers. Make the reasoning visible: say why each
   step follows from the one before, above all where you split a number, use a rule, or go from objects to
   numbers. "`8 + 2 = 10`. Then `10 + 3 = 13`." lists two calculations. It does not say why the 5 became 2 and 3.
   Explain as much as the idea is hard, and no more. The reader should see the idea work before they touch
   anything.
3. **Hand over the figure.** Say what is on the figure, what to try, and what to watch for. When there are a few
   things to try, make them a short list. Explain a little here too when the reader needs it to make sense of what
   they see.

A reader should be able to learn the idea from the first two parts with the figure out of sight.

This order is the default, and it serves the reader. Change it when the page needs something else:

- A page about a real situation opens on the situation, then works one case of it.
- A challenge opens on what the reader has to do and the idea that gets them there. Its example is a case the page
  cannot draw, so the text does not give an answer away.
- The last page of a story opens on what the story has shown.

Let the parts run into each other where that reads better. Three stiff blocks are not the goal.

### When the example and the figure show different things

Sometimes the example is about apples in a bowl and the figure shows counters. That is fine, and schools do it all
the time. But tell the reader, before the first step. They need to know what a thing on the figure stands for, and
what it means in the example when they do something to it. At the end, say the answer in the example's words again.

Page "Take counters away and count what is left" does it like this: "The figure shows the bowl with counters in
place of apples. Each red counter stands for one apple, and taking a counter away is eating that apple." How you
say it is up to you. What matters is that the reader is never left to guess that a counter is an apple.

## How long

Aim for the shortest text that still teaches the idea. The budget is 210 words, and `kitap check` counts them.

For each sentence, ask whether the reader would find the idea harder to understand without it. If they would, it
stays, even when the figure shows something similar. A figure shows that a thing happens. Only the text can say why.

When a text is too long, cut in this order:

1. A preview of later pages or later stories.
2. A second worked example. One case is enough to show the idea.
3. The same idea said twice.
4. A sentence that only reads a label or a readout back to the reader.
5. A step for a control whose label already says what it does, such as Roll.

Keep these, even when space is tight:

- The reasoning in the worked example.
- The answer to a question the text itself asks.
- How to undo a step, when the figure does not make that obvious.

Never squeeze a sentence to save words. Drop a whole sentence instead.

If the idea honestly needs more than 210 words, write the text it needs and say so in your report, with the reason
in one sentence. Whoever runs the story then lists the page under `long` in `plugins/checks/text.yaml`. Do not edit
that file yourself. An exception is for a hard idea. It is not for a text that repeats itself.

## About the samples in this file

A sample shows what a rule means on one page. Write your own sentence for your own page, with its numbers and its
objects. Do not take a sample and change the numbers. Two pages of one story should not teach with the same
sentence or hand over the figure with the same sentence.

Here is one finished text, "Make ten first", so you can see the whole shape. Read it for how it sounds, and do not
reuse its sentences.

> An addition crosses ten when its first number is below 10 and its sum is above 10. Making ten turns it into two
> easy steps. You split the second number into the part that makes ten with the first number, and the rest.
>
> Take `8 + 5`. 8 needs 2 more to make 10, so you split 5 into 2 and 3. The first step is `8 + 2 = 10`. The 3 is the
> rest, and a full ten and 3 more is 13, so the second step is `10 + 3 = 13`.
>
> The first frame has 8 round red counters and 2 empty cells, and 5 square blue counters are in the tray.
>
> - Drag 2 blue counters into the first frame and the other 3 into the second. A tap works too, since it fills the
>   next empty cell.
> - Once the first frame is full and the sum is past 10, the two steps appear under the number sentence.
> - If you drop a blue counter in the second frame while the first frame still has an empty cell, there is no full
>   ten, so no steps appear. Tap that counter to send it back to the tray, then drag it into the first frame.

## The book's conventions

These keep the book consistent. `kitap check` tests a few of them. It fails a text that speaks of the page, has
an em dash or a semicolon, or goes over the word budget. It also prints notes, on a sentence two pages share or a
control named in the first paragraph. A note is for you to weigh: fix what is a real fault, and leave good prose
as it is.

**Terms**

- Use the word schools use, in the form and with the meaning `agents/glossary.md` gives it. The glossary has "sum"
  for what an addition gives, so write "the sum is 8" and not "the total" or "the answer".
- Bring in a term when it helps the reader name an idea and use it again. Explain it in everyday words the first
  time, as part of the explanation. Then stick to it.
- If a glossary term reads like a label somebody made up, or its meaning does not fit your page, say so in your
  report and do not force it in.

**Values that change**

- A value that changes as the reader plays is a link: `[14](@point)`. Put these in the part about the figure. Every
  other number is plain.
- The sentence around a link has to stay true for every value the reader can reach. `kitap check` only tests the
  starting value. "The scale now shows [7](@right)" stays true. "The right pan holds [7](@right), which is odd"
  does not.
- Do not put the noun it counts after a link. "Holds [1](@n) counters" goes wrong at 1. Write "The Counters readout
  now shows [1](@n)."
- The readouts on the figure already show every value. Link one in the text only when it helps to see it change
  among the words. One or two links go in a sentence. Three or more go in a list, one to a line.

**How things are written down**

- A calculation or a number sentence in signs goes between backticks, so it stands out and stays on one line:
  `` `5 + 3 = 8` ``, `` `9 − 4` ``, `` `7 < 9` ``. A number alone, a sign alone, and a number sentence in words
  ("5 plus 3 is equal to 8") stay plain. A link takes no backticks.
- Name a control by the words the figure writes on it, with the name first: "the Jumps readout", "the "Top row"
  stepper". A name of two words or more goes in quotes.
- Name a part of the figure by where it is, its shape, or what is written on it: "the top row", "the left block".
  Some readers cannot tell two colours apart, so a colour can be added to such a name and never replaces it.
- Name another page of the story by its title, in quotes.
- Leave the page and the screen out. The text is about the maths and the figure.
- No em dashes, no semicolons, no middle dots.

**Sentences and lists**

- Explanations are full sentences, each with a subject and a verb.
- When you list things, pick what reads more easily. Short items sit in a sentence with commas. Longer items, or
  several steps to try, read better as a list. Sentences that build on each other stay a paragraph, because a list
  would cut the "so" between them.
- A sentence introduces a list. Each item is a full sentence, or finishes the sentence that introduces it.

**Titles**

- A title is a short plain sentence or a plain name that a person would say: "Every jump adds the same step". A
  challenge's title is what the reader has to do: "Put the point on the number".

**The story**

- A story shows where its idea is used in the real world. A story that follows another one uses new situations.
- The level says what the reader already knows. Do not name an age or a school year.

The words on the figure itself, its goal line, hints, notes and labels, follow this file and "The words on a figure"
in `../hesap/AGENTS.md`.
