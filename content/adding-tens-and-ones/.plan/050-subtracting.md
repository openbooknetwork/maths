## Content

### 510 Subtract the tens and the ones
To subtract a two-digit number, split it into its tens and its ones. Take the tens away from the tens and the ones
away from the ones, and put together what is left in each place. The tens or the ones can go first, and what is left
is the same. This works as adding place by place does, with minus in place of plus.

Example: 68 − 25. 68 is 6 tens and 8 ones, and 25 splits into 2 tens and 5 ones. 6 tens take away 2 tens leave 4
tens, written 60 − 20 = 40. 8 ones take away 5 ones leave 3 ones, written 8 − 5 = 3. 4 tens and 3 ones make 43, so
68 − 25 = 43. Taking the 5 cubes first and the 2 rods after leaves the same 4 rods and 3 cubes.

The rods and cubes on the chart are 68. The pieces taken go to the pile "Taken away", and each leaves a dashed
outline in its column, so the column still shows what 68 held.

### 520 Break a ten to subtract
A number's ones are not only its extra ones. Each of its tens is made of ten ones, so a number has ones inside its
tens too. Where the extra ones are fewer than the ones to take away, break one ten into ten ones first. The number
stays the same, and then the ones and the tens are taken away as before. Breaking first and then taking away is the
order to keep.

Example: 82 − 47. 47 splits into 4 tens and 7 ones. 82 has 2 extra ones, fewer than the 7 ones to take away. Its
other 80 ones are inside its 8 tens. Break one ten: 82 is 7 tens and 12 ones. 12 ones take away 7 ones leave 5 ones,
12 − 7 = 5. 7 tens take away 4 tens leave 3 tens, 70 − 40 = 30. So 82 − 47 = 35.

The error to name: taking the smaller digit from the greater in each place, 8 − 4 = 4 and 7 − 2 = 5, gives 45. The 7
ones belong to 47, the number taken away, so the 7 is taken from the ones of 82, and 45 is wrong. The text says this
with the numbers and leaves out "not enough ones".

### 530 Count on through the ten after
A difference can be found by counting on from the smaller number to the greater, as with numbers up to 20. Counting
on finds the answer of a subtraction too, and it goes round a ten that taking away would break. With two-digit numbers,
count on in three steps: to the ten after the smaller number, then in tens to the ten before the greater number, then
in ones. The three steps add to the difference.

Example, framed as a comparison: Mia has 75 marbles and Sam has 46. How many more does Mia have? "How many more"
asks for the difference, which is the subtraction 75 − 46. The word "more" can lead a reader to add, and 75 + 46
counts both children's marbles together, so the text warns against it. Start on 46. 4 takes it to 50, the ten after.
20 takes 50 to 70. 5 takes 70 to 75. 4 + 20 + 5 = 29, so 75 − 46 = 29 and Mia has 29 more. On the figure the ring on
46 is Sam's marbles and the square on 75 is Mia's.

### 620 Stickers used on two days
A sticker album holds 9 sheets of ten star stickers and 5 single stickers, 95 stickers. A sheet is a ten and a single
sticker is a one. On the first day 42 stickers go on birthday cards, and on the second day 28 go on a poster. How
many stickers are left in the album?

The first day takes 4 sheets and 2 stickers: 9 − 4 = 5 sheets and 5 − 2 = 3 stickers, so 95 − 42 = 53. The second
day takes 2 sheets and 8 stickers. The 3 single stickers are fewer than the 8 to use, and the other stickers are on
the sheets, so a sheet is cut into ten stickers: 53 is 4 sheets and 13 stickers. 13 − 8 = 5 stickers and 4 − 2 = 2
sheets, so 53 − 28 = 25. 25 stickers are left.

The note beside the album holds the two days' numbers, and each envelope holds what one day used.

### 810 What comes after adding tens and ones
The end page goes over the story's ideas, one sentence each:
- Ones added to a number change its ones and keep its tens, until the ones pass ten and the number crosses into the
  ten after.
- Ten more makes the tens digit go up by 1 and ten less makes it go down by 1.
- Two numbers add tens to tens and ones to ones.
- Ten ones of an addition make a new ten, which is counted with the other tens.
- A subtraction takes tens from tens and ones from ones, and breaks a ten where the extra ones are fewer than the
  ones to take away.
- A difference can be counted on through the ten after.

Example: 45 + 37. The tens make 40 + 30 = 70 and the ones make 5 + 7 = 12. 12 ones are 1 new ten and 2 ones, so
45 + 37 = 82. The ones column shows the 12 cubes that a new ten comes from.

The stories that come next:
- **Money** adds prices in pence and counts on to find the change.
- **Adding and subtracting in columns** writes the numbers one under the other and the new ten as a small digit over
  the tens, for numbers up to thousands.

## Interactivity

### 510 Subtract the tens and the ones
- Shows: the chart with 6 red rods and 8 red cubes, the pile "Taken away" beside it with a rod and a cube at 0, and
  no line under the chart yet.
- The reader: drags the tens column twice and the ones column five times, or taps them, in either order.
- The figure: each piece taken goes to the pile and leaves its dashed outline in its column. The counts under the
  columns go down. Once a rod is taken, the line of the tens shows under the chart and follows each rod, to "60 − 20 =
  40". Once a cube is taken, the line of the ones shows and follows each cube, to "8 − 5 = 3". The whole line over
  both follows each piece, to "68 − 25 = 43". A tap on the pile's rod or
  cube puts one back.
- Action: take pieces from both columns, and the line of each place writes what its column has left.

### 520 Break a ten to subtract
- Shows: the chart with 8 rods and 2 cubes, the pile at 0, and the Bundle button.
- The reader: takes the 2 cubes, or starts by tapping the tens column, which breaks its last rod into ten cubes, then
  takes 7 cubes and 4 rods. A tap on the ones column takes a cube, and a tap on the tens column breaks a rod, so the
  rods are dragged off.
- The figure: the broken rod's ten cubes glide into the ones column, and the count under it reads 12. The pieces
  taken leave outlines and fill the pile. The place lines read the columns as they stand, "70 − 40 = 30" and
  "12 − 7 = 5", and the whole line "82 − 47 = 35". A reader who takes the 2 cubes first sees the ones column empty,
  breaks a ten, and reaches the same lines, since the ones line counts the 2 outlines with the cubes on.
- Action: break a ten in the middle of a take away, and take the ones from the ten broken.

### 530 Count on through the ten after
- Shows: a number line from 30 to 80 in ones, labelled at the tens, with the start's ring on 46 and the square second
  point on 75, each with its number in a flag. The buttons "Size of a jump" read 1 and 10, with 1 picked. Under the
  line, "75 − 46" waits for its answer.
- The reader: presses Jump right four times to land on 50, picks 10 and jumps twice to 70, picks 1 and jumps
  five times to 75.
- The figure: each run of jumps gets a tag that grows with each jump, "+4" over the first run, "+20" over the jumps of
  10 and "+5" over the last run. Once the point is on 75 the line reads "75 − 46 = 29". Jump left takes back the last
  jump of either size. The reader can drag the second point to another number and count on again.
- Action: jump from the smaller number to the greater in ones, then tens, then ones, and the tags add to the
  difference.

### 620 Stickers used on two days

  names the column that is off. A chart with the right number and 10 or more cubes is told to bundle ten ones. The
  hint: "27 has 7 ones, more than the 1 extra one of 61. The other ones of 61 are inside its tens, so break a ten: 61

- Hidden: the counts under the columns, the values, the number name, the steppers, every
  readout and the written line. Once the goal holds the number and the line "61 − 34 = 27" show.
- Idea used: breaking a ten where the extra ones are fewer than the ones to take away, together with taking the tens
