# Terms of Skip counting

File names without a folder are in `content/skip-counting/.references/`.

## 010 twos
- Introduces:
  - `skip count (numbers)`: count forwards or back in jumps of one number other than 1, as in twos, fives, tens or
    hundreds. "Skip counting" names the story's idea, and a page that counts in one size says which, as "count in
    twos". Oak gives the reader "Skip count - Counting forwards or backwards by a number other than 1."
    (`thenational.academy-ary-ks1-units-composition-of-numbers-6-to-10-lessons-skip-count-in-odds-and-evens.txt`,
    Keywords). Written without a hyphen as Oak has it, over the US "skip-count by 5s"
    (`.cache/curricula/us-common-core-math.pdf`, 2.NBT.A.2), since the book takes England's forms.
  - `count in twos, fives, tens, hundreds (numbers)`: from a start, say the numbers that each add 2 (5, 10, 100) to
    the one before, as 2, 4, 6, 8 from 0, or 10, 12, 14 from 8. To count back in twos is to say the numbers that each
    subtract 2, as 14, 12, 10. Each number said is one jump of 2 on the line. A new sense beside
    `count in tens, fives, twos (number line)`, which is said of a line's labels. England gives "count in multiples of
    twos, fives and tens" (`.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf`, page 6) and "count in ones, tens and hundreds" (same
    file, page 18). Chosen over the US "count by 2s" as the book's British form, the form the glossary already has.
    This section teaches the twos, and 020, 030 and 040 the others.
  - `jump of 2, 5, 10, 100 (number line)`: a jump that adds 2 (5, 10, 100), drawn as one arc however many marks it
    crosses. The number it adds is the size of the jump, so a jump of 5 has the size 5. A jump back of 5, to the left,
    subtracts 5. The glossary's `jump (number line)` holds as written, and "of 5" gives its size. Oak gives "When you
    count in odds or evens you count in jumps of two."
    (`thenational.academy-ary-ks1-units-composition-of-numbers-6-to-10-lessons-skip-count-in-odds-and-evens.txt`, Key
    learning points), and Teppo "the initial jump of ‘‘5’’"
    (`content/number-line/.references/fisme.science.uu.nl-publicaties-literatuur-2013-teppo-number-line-zdm.txt`,
    4.1). Chosen over England's "count in steps of 2" (`.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf`, page 11), because
    `step (number line)` is what a jump to the next mark adds, and a line with a mark for every number has the step 1
    while its jumps are of 2.
  - `equal (jumps)`: of the same size, so each jump adds the same number, and on a line with equal gaps each jump has
    the same length. A new sense beside `equal (gaps)` and `equal (numbers)`, in the brief's words "a count in equal
    jumps". No source read has the phrase. The sources give "jumps of two" for the same thing.
  - `group of 2, 5, 10, 100 (objects)`: a group with that many objects, which a count in twos (fives, tens,
    hundreds) counts with one jump, as the 2 wheels of a bike or a box of 10 pencils. The figure draws one group above
    each jump. Oak gives "Groups of 2 - Groups of two objects that can be counted in twos."
    (`thenational.academy-recognitions-counting-in-2s-5s-and-10s-lessons-count-efficiently-in-groups-of-two.txt`,
    Keywords) and "Group of 5 - Group of five objects that can be counted in fives."
    (`thenational.academy-n-recognitions-counting-in-2s-5s-and-10s-lessons-count-efficiently-in-groups-of-5.txt`,
    Keywords). The groups of 5, 10 and 100 come in 020, 030 and 040.
  - `pair (things that come in twos)`: two things of one kind that are used together, such as two socks, which is a
    group of 2. A third sense beside `pair (objects)` of the matching stories and `pair (numbers)`, which this story
    does not use. The opening page's socks are "pegged in pairs" in the brief, and Oak gives "Numbers that can be
    made out of pairs are even numbers."
    (`thenational.academy-ary-ks1-units-composition-of-numbers-6-to-10-lessons-skip-count-in-odds-and-evens.txt`, Key
    learning points).
  - `even, odd (numbers)`: a number is even when that many objects can be put in groups of 2 with none left over, and
    odd when one is left over. The count in twos from 0 lands on the even numbers, 2, 4, 6, and the count in twos
    from 1 lands on the odd numbers it jumped over, 3, 5, 7. 0 is even. England gives "recognise odd and even numbers,
    up to 10, based on whether they can be composed of groups of 2 or not"
    (`assets.publishing.service.gov.uk-ads-system-uploads-attachment-data-file-954511-Maths-guidance-year-1.txt`,
    1AS–1 Teaching guidance), and Oak "Even - Any number that can be divided exactly by 2. The last digit is 0, 2, 4,
    6 or 8." (`thenational.academy-ary-ks1-units-composition-of-numbers-6-to-10-lessons-skip-count-in-odds-and-evens.txt`,
    Keywords). The definition takes England's groups of 2, since the reader does not know division.
- Uses: `count on, count back (numbers)`, `number line (figure)`, `mark (number line)`, `label (number line)`,
  `point (number line)`, `gap (number line)`, `jump (number line)`, `start (number line)`,
  `to the right, to the left (number line)`, `add (numbers)`, `subtract (numbers)`, `ten, one (numbers)`

## 030 fives
- Introduces:
  - `count in twos, fives, tens, hundreds (numbers)` for the fives, forward and back: 5, 10, 15 from 0, and 45, 40,
    35 back from 50.
  - `group of 2, 5, 10, 100 (objects)` for the group of 5, as the five fingers of a hand.
  - `multiple (numbers)`: a multiple of 5 is 0 or a number that the count in fives from 0 lands on, 5, 10, 15 and on,
    and so for 2, 10 and 100. The multiples of 2 are the even numbers. A count from another start lands on other
    numbers, so the count in twos from 1 lands on no multiple of 2. England gives "count in multiples of twos, fives
    and tens" (`.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf`, page 6) and "Counting in multiples of 2 (even numbers)"
    (`assets.publishing.service.gov.uk-ads-system-uploads-attachment-data-file-954511-Maths-guidance-year-1.txt`,
    1NF–2 Making connections), and Oak gives the reader "Fives - Another name for the multiples of five."
    (`thenational.academy-n-recognitions-counting-in-2s-5s-and-10s-lessons-count-efficiently-in-groups-of-5.txt`,
    Keywords). Oak defines it as "the result of multiplying a number by another whole number"
    (`thenational.academy-10-times-tables-lessons-explain-the-relationship-between-adjacent-multiples-of-5.txt`,
    Keywords), and the entry gives it by the count, which picks out the same numbers and needs no multiplying. Taken
    in because every English source names the numbers a count lands on so, and the story needs a noun for them from
    020 to 040.
  - `digit, last digit (numbers)`: a digit is one of the ten signs 0 to 9 that numbers are written with, so 45 has
    the two digits 4 and 5. The last digit is the one on the right, the 5 of 45, and "the last two digits" are the two
    on the right, the 75 of 275. "Ends in 5" says the last digit is 5. Oak gives "The last digit is 1, 3, 5, 7 or 9."
    (`thenational.academy-ary-ks1-units-composition-of-numbers-6-to-10-lessons-skip-count-in-odds-and-evens.txt`,
    Keywords) and "When counting in fives, each number ends in either 0 or 5"
    (`thenational.academy-ions-counting-in-2s-5s-and-10s-lessons-skip-count-in-fives-forwards-and-backwards.txt`, Key
    learning points). Chosen over Oak's "ones digit", which needs the place value of `tens-and-ones`.
  - `hundred square (figure)`: a square of 100 cells in ten rows of ten, with the numbers 1 to 100 in the order of the
    count, left to right along each row and row after row, so each row ends with a ten. A count lights the cells it
    lands on. England gives "pupils can point to numerals on a number line or 100 square"
    (`assets.publishing.service.gov.uk-ads-system-uploads-attachment-data-file-954511-Maths-guidance-year-1.txt`, page
    19, 1NF–2 Teaching guidance), and Oak "one hundred squares"
    (`thenational.academy-ns-to-and-from-2-digit-numbers-lessons-find-10-more-or-less-than-a-2-digit-number.txt`,
    Common misconception). Written in words as the book writes "ten frame", and in England's form over the US
    "hundreds chart". After a page names it, "the square" refers back to it.
  - `cell (hundred square)`: one place of the hundred square, which holds one number. A new sense beside
    `cell (ten frame)`, in the same word, so "square" stays the name of the whole figure.
  - `row, column (hundred square)`: a row is ten cells side by side, one ten of the count, 1 to 10, 11 to 20 and on.
    A column is ten cells one under another, whose numbers have the same last digit, each 10 more than the one above
    it. The last column is the one on the right, 10, 20 and on to 100. The US progression gives "the numerals from 1
    to 120 can be shown in columns of 10 to help highlight the base-ten structure"
    (`isbe.net-Documents-number-base-ten-k-5.txt`, page 7).
- Uses: `skip count (numbers)`, `jump of 2, 5, 10, 100 (number line)`, `equal (jumps)`, `even, odd (numbers)`,
  `count on, count back (numbers)`, `number line (figure)`, `mark (number line)`, `jump (number line)`,
  `start (number line)`, `to the right, to the left (number line)`, `ten, one (numbers)`, `add (numbers)`,
  `subtract (numbers)`

## 040 hundreds
- Introduces:
  - `count in twos, fives, tens, hundreds (numbers)` for the hundreds, 100, 200, 300 and on to 1000, from 0 and
    from any start, as 75, 175, 275.
  - `group of 2, 5, 10, 100 (objects)` for the group of 100, as a pack of 100 sheets of paper.
  - `Size of a jump (figure)`, the engine's existing name: the control where the reader picks the size of every jump, 2, 5, 10 or 100. No
    source names a control for it. The name follows the entry `jump of 2, 5, 10, 100 (number line)`, whose number is
    the size of the jump.
- Uses: `skip count (numbers)`, `jump of 2, 5, 10, 100 (number line)`, `equal (jumps)`, `multiple (numbers)`,
  `digit, last digit (numbers)`, `ten, one (numbers)`, `number line (figure)`, `mark (number line)`,
  `label (number line)`, `point (number line)`, `start (number line)`, `add (numbers)`, `subtract (numbers)`

## Rules
- "Skip count" names the idea. A page that counts in one size says "count in twos", "count in fives", "count in
  tens" or "count in hundreds", and the count the other way is "count back in fives". "Forwards" and "backwards" are
  left out of the terms, as the glossary has "count on" and "count back".
- "Count back" alone is the glossary's count back one by one. "Count back in fives" says the jumps are of 5.
- "A line counts in tens" is said of the labels on a line, from `number-line`. "You count in twos" is said of the
  jumps. A line with a mark for every number counts in ones while its jumps are of 2, and the text then names the
  jumps.
- The size of a jump is a number. A jump of 10 is bigger than a jump of 2, and 10 is greater than 2. Jumps are more
  and fewer, so "the bigger the jumps, the fewer you need".
- The step is what a jump to the next mark adds, and the size of a jump is what the jump adds. On a line with a mark
  every 10, the line has the step 10 while its jumps have the size 100, and the text says "a jump of 100".
- A jump "lands on" a number. The brief's noun "landing" is left out of the reader's text, which says "the numbers
  you land on".
- The start is not a jump, as the glossary has it. The jumps from 0 to 10 in twos are five, and the count says five
  numbers, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. To find how many jumps, count the arcs and not the marks.
- A jump "adds 2" and a jump back "subtracts 2". The reader "makes a jump" and "jumps back", and the text does not
  say "add a jump" or "take a jump away", since add is said of numbers and take away of groups.
- The number of jumps is the number of groups, and the number the last jump from 0 lands on is how many objects
  there are altogether. 3 groups of 2 are 6 socks, and 3 is the number of groups. "Total" is left out, as the
  glossary rules say.
- A group is the small group one jump counts. All the objects together are named by what they are, "the socks" or
  "all the wheels", and "set" is left out.
- "Pair" is said of things that come in twos, such as socks. The maths word is "group of 2", and even and odd are
  told by groups of 2. The matching sense, a pair joined by a pair line, is not used in this story.
- 0 is even and a multiple of every number. Oak's teacher tip "Zero is neither odd nor even" is wrong and left out.
  The text need not raise 0, and does not say that 0 is not even.
- Up to 100 the multiples of 10 are "the tens", as the glossary's `ten, one (numbers)` has it. Past 100 they are
  "multiples of 10", as 380 and 390.
- "Previous" and "next multiple", "decade number", "100s boundary" and "unitising" are left out. The tens either side
  of a number are "the ten before" and "the ten after", as the glossary has it.
- On the hundred square a count "lights" the cells it lands on. "Mark" is said only of a number line, and a place on
  the square is a cell, so "square" names the whole figure.
- "Ends in 5" and "the last digit is 5" say the same. A jump of 10 keeps the last digit and the digit before it goes
  up by one, and on the hundred square it lands on the cell just below. A one digit start such as 3 gets a 1 written
  before it, 13. A jump of 100 keeps the last two digits.
- 1000 is written without a separator, as the brief and the line's labels have it. The separator of four digit
  numbers is left to `hundreds-and-thousands`.
- The number a challenge asks the reader to land on is "the target", an everyday word that is not a term.
- Left to later stories:
  - `tens-and-ones` introduces "ones digit" and "tens digit" and what a digit is worth. This story says "last digit".
  - `adding-tens-and-ones` introduces "10 more" and "10 less", which its rough map says. This story says a jump of 10
    adds 10.
  - `equal-groups` introduces "equal groups" and the multiplication view of a multiple, which picks out the same
    numbers as the count.
  - `hundreds-and-thousands` introduces a hundred as ten tens, the hundreds digit and the thousand.
  - `number-patterns` decides "pattern" and "sequence". This story names the counts and leaves "pattern" as an
    everyday word.


- The brief teaches the hundred square, its cells, rows and columns in 020 `tens`, where the tens fill the last
  column. Where this file lists them under `fives`, they come first in `tens`.
