# Research for Fact families

The files named below sit in `content/fact-families/.references/`, except the curricula, which sit in
`.cache/curricula/`. Page numbers of PDFs are the pages of the PDF. In the isbe.net file the page of the PDF is one
less than the page number printed at its foot. The IM scope file writes its equations in TeX, as in `\(8 + 2 = 10\)`,
and the quotes keep that form. The isbe.net file drops the signs +, − and = from its equations, so its quotes leave
those out. The Lesson Study Alliance file is a grade 2 unit plan for a US school, built on the Japanese textbook
series it calls IES.

What the curricula say, for all sections:

## 010 four-facts

- Order: three numbers first, then the two additions, then the two subtractions. "As a whole class, the teacher
  presents students with 3 numbers on cards, such as 5, 8, and 3." and "The teacher then points out that the next
  equation is subtraction and asks the students to find a subtraction equation using the same three numbers."
  (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt, Actions).
- Order: the family comes after practice within 10. "It is best given after the students have had quite a bit of
  experience adding and subtracting within 10." (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt,
  IM Commentary).
- Order: the swap of the parts comes before the subtractions. NCETM's Year 1 unit lists "Pupils explain that addends
  can be represented in any order. This is called the commutative law" as outcome 2 and "Pupils partition a whole
  into two parts and express this with a subtraction equation" as outcome 7
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-cp-year-1-unit-6-additive-structures.txt, Learning outcomes). The NCETM lessons
  teach the swap with a whole of two kinds: "adding the number of two adult cats and four kittens" and "we can write
  the addends in either order, but we still have six cats altogether."
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-8d8533d695aa6c1-pvltg-ks1-number-addition-subtraction-pt1-1-to-5.txt, Lesson 1).
- Order: the counters in a ten frame give the four facts at once. "this 10-frame may allow students to recall several
  related facts: \(8 + 2 = 10\) \(2 + 8 = 10\) \(10 - 2 = 8\) \(10 - 8 = 2\)"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 3, Section A).
- Order: one picture gives the equations of a part-whole model first, then the subtraction. Oak asks "Sofia writes
  four equations to match a picture. 6 = 4 + 2 4 + 2 = 6 2 + 4 = 6 6 = 2 + 4"
  (thenational.academy-teachers-lessons-understanding-part-part-whole-relationships.txt, Starter quiz Q6), and in a
  later lesson "Jun uses different coloured counters to represent the two parts in a story. He uses 4 red counters and
  6 white counters." with the answers "10 - 4 = 6" and "4 + 6 = 10"
  (thenational.academy-res-addition-lessons-understand-the-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt, Exit
  quiz Q6).
- Order: Eureka Math builds the families from an addition chart near the end of its first grade 1 module. "Pick an
  addition fact from the chart. Use the grid to write the two subtraction facts that would have the same number
  bond." (onlinemathlearning.com-relate-addition-subtraction-facts.txt, Lesson 39 Homework).
- Examples: 5, 8 and 3 written as "5 + 3 = 8 3 + 5 = 8 8 - 5 = 3 8 - 3 = 5"
  (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt, Actions).
- Examples: a whole of one thing in two kinds. "There are six presents, two have bows on them and the rest do not."
  and "There are two deflated balloons and six inflated balloons." (ncetm.org.uk-media-yoyphd14-pska-add-q1.txt, Did
  you notice that…?). "there are apples and bananas in a fruit bowl, or children standing up and sitting down" (same
  file, Aggregation (addition) and partitioning (subtraction) structures).
- Examples: a game with two colour counters. "Elena is playing Shake and Spill. She has 7 counters. What are some
  ways to show some red and some yellow?" and "the commutative property (\(4+3=7\) and \(3+4=7\))"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2, Section B).
- Examples: beads on a string. "Tick two equations that represent the beads on the bead string." with the answers
  "5 - 2 = 3" and "2 + 3 = 5"
  (thenational.academy-res-addition-lessons-understand-the-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt, Exit
  quiz Q4).
- Examples: a known sum gives two subtractions. "if they know that 9 + 7 = 16 and understand inversion, they can use
  this knowledge to answer two more questions: 16 – 9 = ? and 16 – 7 = ?"
  (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 25).
- Words: "fact families". "these are often known as "fact families" because the equations are related by the same
  underlying relationship between the numbers."
  (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt, IM Commentary).
- Words: "related facts" and "related subtraction facts". "knowing an addition fact can help us know the related
  subtraction facts" (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt,
  Unit 8, Section A). England: "number bonds and related subtraction facts within 20" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf,
  page 7). Eureka: "sets of related addition and subtraction facts" (onlinemathlearning.com-relate-addition-subtraction-facts.txt).
- Words: "related equations". "Each addition and subtraction equation has seven related equations."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 18, Grade 2).
- Words: "whole" and "part", in a stem sentence. "___ is the whole, ___ is a part and ___ is a part"
  (thenational.academy-to-10-lessons-identify-a-missing-part-when-a-whole-is-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt, Common
  misconception).
- Words: the IM task leaves out the name of the swap. "students do not need to use the terms “commutative property”
  or “associative property.” These are referred to as the “add in any order” property."
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2, Section B).
- Errors: the part first in a subtraction. "one can determine their depth of understanding by noting whether or not
  the students use the minuend to begin the equation or the subtrahend." The advice: "cubes or manipulatives can be
  provided to model the equations" and "the same numbers can each be circled in a particular color"
  (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt, IM Commentary).
- Errors: the signs mixed up. "Particularly at the beginning of first grade some students may confuse the +, -, and =
  symbols." (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt, Actions).
- Errors: the subtractions are harder than the additions. "Creating the two addition equations will be easier for most
  children." (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt, IM Commentary).
- Errors: a known sum is not used for its subtraction. "These studies established that the step from the first to
  the second sum is extremely difficult for children in their first two or three years at school, and most of them
  fail to take it." (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 25, the complement question).
- Errors: a part-whole picture shows three numbers at once. "Students sometimes have trouble with static part-whole
  diagrams because these display a double representation of the total and the addends" and "The action of moving from
  the total to the addends (or from the addends to the total) in the number-bond diagram reduces this conceptual
  difficulty." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 16).
- Errors: counters in the parts and the whole at the same time. "When using part-part-whole models, children may put
  counters in the parts and whole simultaneously." The advice: "Work practically, moving counters to show we can
  partition the whole into parts and recombine the parts to make the whole again."
  (thenational.academy-teachers-lessons-understanding-part-part-whole-relationships.txt, Common misconception).
- Errors: a whole with no two clear kinds. "Discuss the partitioning structure of subtraction in terms of one part
  having a characteristic and one part not having that characteristic to avoid children becoming confused if the
  whole group does not have two clearly identifiable parts."
  (thenational.academy-res-addition-lessons-understand-the-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt, Teacher
  tip).
- Errors: facts learnt one by one. "Children spend much time at home and in school practising number facts, perhaps
  trying to memorise them as if they were independent of each other." (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf,
  page 25).

## 020 undoing

- Order: Oak teaches undoing at the end of its Year 1 unit, after parts and wholes and the missing part. NCETM's unit
  has "Pupils work out the missing part of a subtraction story and equation if the other two parts are known" as
  outcome 16 and "Pupils explain that addition and subtraction are inverse operations (1)" as outcome 17
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-cp-year-1-unit-6-additive-structures.txt, Learning outcomes).
- Order: a story with a start, a change and an end, then the change undone. "When a given amount is added to the start
  of a story, to 'undo' the change, we must subtract the same amount." and "When a given amount is subtracted from the
  start of a story, to 'undo' the change we must add the same amount."
  (thenational.academy-and-subtraction-lessons-know-that-addition-and-subtraction-are-inverse-operations.txt, Key
  learning points).
- Order: acting out first, then pictures, then equations. "first ask children to act out scenarios, then represent
  pictorially alongside." (thenational.academy-and-subtraction-lessons-know-that-addition-and-subtraction-are-inverse-operations.txt,
  Teacher tip).
- Order: the same things added and taken back before the same number. "children’s understanding of the inversion of
  identity precedes, and may provide the basis for, their understanding of the inversion of quantity. First they
  understand that adding and subtracting the same stuff restores the physical status quo. Then they extend this
  knowledge to quantity" (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, pages 24 and 25).
- Order: objects before numbers alone. "The fact that young children are readier to use the inversion principle in
  concrete than in abstract problems suggests that they may learn about inversion initially through their actions
  with concrete material." (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 24). A taught group used bricks
  under a cloth: "First the children counted the number of bricks in a row of Unifix bricks, which was subsequently
  hidden under a cloth" and "the experimenter added some bricks to the row and subtracted others." This group did
  better on new kinds of problems: "the Visual Demonstration method was effective in promoting transfer" (same file,
  pages 25 and 26).
- Order: a number line shows the undoing. Oak's exit quiz asks "What number is missing from the number line?"
  (thenational.academy-ction-lessons-represent-the-inverse-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt, Exit quiz
  Q2), and the earlier digest has NCETM's "They can start at a fixed point on a number line and will either count on
  (augmentation) or back (reduction)." (content/adding-within-20/.references/ncetm.org.uk-media-pkxnnz0r-pska-add-q2.txt).
- Order: checking with the inverse is a Year 2 statement in England and an end of year 4 standard in Bremen. "use this
  to check calculations" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, page 12). "kontrollieren Lösungen durch geeignete
  Vorgehensweisen (z. B. Überschlagsrechnung, Umkehroperation)." (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf,
  page 40).
- Examples: the inverse as a law with numbers. "equal additions and subtractions cancel each other out: 27 + 19 – 19 =
  27 and 27 – 19 + 19 = 27." (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 22).
- Examples: everyday undoing. "A child gets his shirt dirty (mud is added to it) and then it is cleaned (mud is
  subtracted) and the shirt is as it was before." and "If I have 10 sweets and someone gives me 3 more and then I eat
  3, I have the same number left as at the start" (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 24).
- Examples: stories in Oak. "First, I had 9 pencils. Then, 2 pencils were taken. Now, I have 7 pencils." undone by "7 +
  2 = 9" (thenational.academy-and-subtraction-lessons-know-that-addition-and-subtraction-are-inverse-operations.txt,
  Exit quiz Q3). "First, there were 8 cakes on my plate. Then, 3 cakes were eaten." undone by "5 + 3 = 8" and
  "Alex collects some conkers. First, he puts 6 conkers in his pocket but then 5 conkers fall out."
  (thenational.academy-ction-lessons-represent-the-inverse-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt, Starter
  quiz Q3, Exit quiz Q3).
- Examples: one picture told both ways. "First I had 3 cubes. Then Alex gave me 6 more. Now I have 9 cubes." and
  "First I had 9 cubes. Then I gave 6 to Aisha. Now I have 3 cubes." with the answer "Both Alex and Aisha are
  correct." (thenational.academy-ction-lessons-represent-the-inverse-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt,
  Exit quiz Q5).
- Examples: a check by working back in Singapore Primary 2. "Mrs Tan had 235 bookmarks. She gave 123 bookmarks to her
  students." then "Work Backwards 2. Using your answer, can you check if the total is 235?"
  (isomer-user-content.by.gov.sg-0Drawing-20for-20Young-20Learners-Mathematics-Parents-20Workshop-202026.txt, Qn 1,
  Triple Check).
- Words: "inverse" and "undo" in Oak Year 1. "Addition is the inverse of subtraction. Addition can 'undo'
  subtraction." and the keyword "Inverse - Inverse means the opposite in effect; the reverse of."
  (thenational.academy-and-subtraction-lessons-know-that-addition-and-subtraction-are-inverse-operations.txt, Key
  learning points, Keywords).
- Words: "inverse operations" in NCETM Year 1. "Pupils explain that addition and subtraction are inverse operations"
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-cp-year-1-unit-6-additive-structures.txt, outcome 17).
- Words: "Umkehraufgaben" and "Umkehroperation" in Bremen (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 40).
  "the difference, which is the inverse" in Japan (.cache/curricula/japan-course-of-study-2008-en.pdf, page 7).
- Errors: the wrong number undone. "Children may look at the numbers but not be able to visualise the context, for
  example, they may try to undo 6 + 4 = 10 with 10 - 6 = 4". The advice: "Use practical equipment to illustrate the
  importance of the number added and subtracted being the same."
  (thenational.academy-ction-lessons-represent-the-inverse-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt, Common
  misconception).
- Errors: the order of the numbers. "Children may not understand the importance of the order of the numerals in
  equations, so may not understand that the amount added or subtracted is of key importance when undoing a change
  made." The advice: "Present errors to discuss and correct. Encourage children to use practical equipment"
  (thenational.academy-and-subtraction-lessons-know-that-addition-and-subtraction-are-inverse-operations.txt, Common
  misconception).
- Errors: a shortcut in place of the idea. After many drills children "often, quite inappropriately, overgeneralised
  the inversion strategy to the control sums: they would give a as the answer in a + b + b control problems as well as
  in inverse a + b – b problems." (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 23). The advice the
  authors draw: problems that need the inverse mixed with problems that do not, with objects and feedback, as in their
  taught group, "The number of bricks added and subtracted was either the same or differed by one" (same file,
  page 25).
- Errors: young children do not see the undoing. "Children in the five- to eight-year range do not see that if 4 + 8 =
  12, therefore 12 – 8 = 4 because they do not realise that the original addition (+8) is cancelled out by the inverse
  subtraction (–8)." This is Piaget's claim, which the authors question with studies that use bricks
  (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 22).

## 030 missing-part

- Order: the missing part is found within 10 before 20. Oak teaches it in its unit on 6 to 10: "If we know one part we
  can find the other part." and "A bar model can be used to represent the whole number and a missing part."
  (thenational.academy-to-10-lessons-identify-a-missing-part-when-a-whole-is-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt, Key
  learning points). NCETM 1.4: "Each of the numbers six to ten can be partitioned into two parts; if we know one part,
  we can find the other part." (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-primm-104-composition-of-numbers-6-10.txt, Teaching
  point 5). IM: "The numbers are kept within 10 so students can focus on interpreting each problem"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2).
- Order: a missing addend first, then the subtraction that finds it. NCETM 1.5: "We can represent problems with
  missing parts using an addition equation with a missing addend." (Teaching point 3) before "the subtraction symbol,
  −, can be used to represent partitioning." (Teaching point 4)
  (ncetm.org.uk-oom-resources-primm-105-additive-structures-introduction-to-aggregation-and-partitioning.txt).
- Order: the missing part is a second way subtraction is the opposite of addition, apart from undoing. "Put
  Together/Take Apart problems with Addend Unknown afford students the opportunity to see subtraction as the opposite of
  addition in a different way than as reversing the action, namely as finding an unknown addend."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 13).
- Order: the equation of the story, then an equation that gives the answer. "To make progress, the student can write
  a related equation called a solution equation—in this case, either 8 l 14 or 14 8 l."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 13, the file drops the signs of "8 + □ = 14" and "14 − 8 =
  □").
- Order: counting on finds the missing part and the subtraction alike. "Whether the problem is 9 4 or 13 9, we will
  hear the student say the same thing: “Niiiiine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen”" and "counting on reinforces that
  subtraction is an unknown-addend problem." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 15).
- Order: a known sum answers the subtraction. "\(9 + \boxed{8} = 17\), so \(17 - 9 = \boxed{8}\)"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 8, Section A).
  Oxford: "Turning subtraction questions into addition questions, as we do in the counting on method, can help
  children to use their addition knowledge to solve subtraction problems."
  (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt, Counting on and
  number bonds).
- Order: the rule that follows. "We use subtraction when we are trying to find a missing part." and the summary "When
  using a tape diagram use addition to find the whole (total) and use subtraction to find a part."
  (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt, Solving and Discussing,
  Summarizing). Oak: "In problems, if the whole and one part of the whole are known, subtraction can be used to find
  the missing part." (thenational.academy-res-addition-lessons-understand-the-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt,
  Key learning points).
- Examples: counters under a cup. "There are 9 counters total. How many counters are under the cup?" written as
  "\(9-4=\boxed{5}\)" or "\(4 +\boxed{5} = 9\)"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2).
  Oak: "Lucas has 7 counters in total. How many are hidden in his hand?"
  (thenational.academy-to-10-lessons-identify-a-missing-part-when-a-whole-is-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt, Exit
  quiz Q2).
- Examples: pennies in two pockets. "I have 6 pennies in my two pockets. 3 pennies are in one pocket. How many are in
  the other pocket?" (thenational.academy-res-addition-lessons-understand-the-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt,
  Starter quiz Q2).
- Examples: what is still to do. "Lucas is going to clap 6 times. He has already clapped 2 times. How many more claps
  does he need to do?", "Jacob is going to hop 8 times. He has already hopped 4 times." and "A ladybird is climbing
  up a flower. The flower is 9 cm tall and the ladybird is 2 cm up. How much further does it need to go?"
  (thenational.academy-to-10-lessons-identify-a-missing-part-when-a-whole-is-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt, Starter
  quiz Q5, Exit quiz Q3 and Q4).
- Examples: a whole of two kinds. "There are one hundred and seventy-two books in the book corner. Fifty-three of the
  books are fiction. How many are non-fiction?" and "if there are seven pieces of fruit in the bowl, five of them are
  bananas and the rest are apples, how many apples are there?" (ncetm.org.uk-media-yoyphd14-pska-add-q1.txt,
  Question 1 and Aggregation and partitioning structures). "There are 60 sheets of colored paper altogether. Of these,
  40 sheets are red and 20 sheets are blue." (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt,
  Problem). "There were 900 people at a concert. 500 were men and the rest are women."
  (isomer-user-content.by.gov.sg-0Drawing-20for-20Young-20Learners-Mathematics-Parents-20Workshop-202026.txt,
  Primary 2).
- Examples: a part added in a story. "Kiran has 6 books. His friend gives him some more books. Now, he has 8 books.",
  solved "by counting on from 6 to 8" as "\(6 + \boxed{2} = 8\)"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2, Section A).
- Examples: things left without a partner. "“If there are 8 trucks and 5 drivers, how many trucks do not have a
  driver?”" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 12).
- Words: "missing part" (Oak, above), "missing addend" (NCETM 1.5, above), "unknown addend" (US 1.OA.B.4, IM). IM's
  sentence for the reader: "subtraction is like finding the value of an unknown addend. If we have to find an unknown
  addend, we can subtract. If we need to subtract, we could think about how much to add to the smaller number."
  (illustrativemathematics.org-wp-content-uploads-2023-10-Grade1-2-Unit-teacher-guide.pdf, Unit 2, Lesson 15,
  printed pages 156 and 157). England uses "missing number problems" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, pages 7 and 12). Bremen calls
  subtraction as a missing part "Ergänzen" (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 21).
- Words: Oak names the numbers of a subtraction and warns about them. "The terms minuend, subtrahend and difference may
  be difficult if only used occasionally." (thenational.academy-res-addition-lessons-understand-the-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt,
  Common misconception). The US progression leaves them out: "Formal vocabulary for subtraction (“minuend” and
  “subtrahend”) is not needed for Kindergarten, Grade 1, and Grade 2" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf,
  page 8).
- Errors: the whole and a part added. "Children may confuse the part and the whole, or add the whole and one part to
  find the missing part." The advice: "Encourage the use of the stem sentence ___ is the whole, ___ is a part and ___
  is a part, to reinforce what the numbers represent."
  (thenational.academy-to-10-lessons-identify-a-missing-part-when-a-whole-is-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt, Common
  misconception). The Lesson Study plan expects the same answer: for the red papers "• 60 – 20 • ____ + 20 = 60 • 60
  + 20" (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt, Anticipated Solutions).
- Errors: the = sign read as "the answer comes next". "They may initially see it as a prompt for the answer to a
  question, which makes it difficult to interpret equations such as \(7=5+2\). Developing an understanding of the equal
  sign is particularly important in solving missing-addend problems."
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2).
- Errors: an equation that does not match the story. "It is more important for students to explain the relationship
  between the equation they wrote and the story, than for their equations to match the story or their solution
  method." (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2).
- Errors: a missing part read as a take away. "children confusing the structures of partitioning and reduction, as
  they tend to be more familiar with the ‘take away’ structure for subtraction."
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-yoyphd14-pska-add-q1.txt, Common errors).
- Errors: counting down. "Taking away objects at the end suggests counting down, which is more difficult than counting
  on." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 15).

## 040 bar

- Order: NCETM's steps from objects to a bar. "To prepare young children for the bar model it is a good idea to
  encourage them to line up objects in a linear arrangement" then "The physical objects can then be replaced, in time,
  with linking cubes and with a bar drawn next to it. The question can then be asked “what’s the same, what’s
  different?”" and "It is useful for children to work in pairs with one manipulating the cubes, while the other
  records by drawing the bars and then writing the number sentence underneath."
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-ca-the-bar-model.txt, Addition and Subtraction).
- Order: NCETM brings in the bar in Year 1, after other pictures. "It should be preceded by and used in conjunction
  with a variety of representations, both concrete and pictorial" and "In the NCETM Primary Professional Development
  Materials they are introduced in Year 1." (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-ca-the-bar-model.txt, Guidance).
- Order: Oak brings in the bar after the part-part-whole model, in its unit on 0 to 5. "Combine the bars to form the
  full bar model, and compare this with the part-part-whole model." and "Encourage children to put small objects in the
  parts and move them into the whole as with the part-part-whole model."
  (thenational.academy-rs-0-to-5-lessons-use-a-bar-model-to-represent-a-whole-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt, Key
  learning points, Teacher tip).
- Order: the Japanese route, from blocks to a paper strip. The lesson opens with blocks, "Clarify the sets by boxing in
  the blocks with lines.", then "How can we represent the butterflies with the strips of paper?", "Where should we cut
  the paper to show the correct size for the brown butterflies?" and "Tell students that this is called a tape
  diagram." The reason: "the concrete block representation, which is limited to small numbers, and the more abstract
  tape diagram, which can be used to represent the larger quantities"
  (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt, About this Lesson, Introduction).
- Order: in Japan the tape diagram comes in grade 1. "In this unit (Grade 1 Unit 14), students will learn addition and

  diagrams and use the diagrams to think about what operation to use"
  (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt, Rationale).
- Order: Maths No Problem, from objects to boxes to one bar. "Pupils will naturally develop from handling concrete
  objects, to drawing pictorial representations, to creating abstract rectangles to illustrate a problem. With time and
  practice, pupils will no longer need to draw individual boxes/units. Instead, they will label one long
  rectangle/bar with a number." (mathsnoproblem.com-en-approach-bar-modelling.txt, Bar modelling and the CPA
  approach).
- Order: in the US the tape diagram comes later in grade 1, first for comparing. "Later in Grade 1, a tape diagram
  can be used." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 12). "The tape diagram shows the addends as the
  tapes and the total (indicated by a bracket) as a composition of those tapes." (same file, page 16).
- Order: Singapore's part-whole model appears in a Primary 2 problem on addition and subtraction within 1000, with the
  comparison model beside it. "Part-Whole Model Shows the various parts that make up a whole" and "Comparison Model
  Shows the relationship between 2 or more quantities when they are compared"
  (isomer-user-content.by.gov.sg-0Drawing-20for-20Young-20Learners-Mathematics-Parents-20Workshop-202026.txt, Types of
  Model Drawing, citing "MOE., (2009). The Singapore Model Method for Learning Mathematics").
- Order: a covered part, then the same bar with each number covered in turn. "Give students a square tile to cover up
  the quantities." and "How are the three tape diagrams different?" with the answers "Each one has a different unknown
  number." and "The square is in a different place."
  (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt, Solving and Discussing).
- Order: one bar holds four facts. "This diagram encapsulates all of the following relationships; a = b + c ; a = c +
  b ; a – b = c ; a – c = b" (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-ca-the-bar-model.txt, Addition and Subtraction). "These
  models can also support the understanding of the relationship between addition and subtraction as they are seen
  within the same representation." (ncetm.org.uk-media-yoyphd14-pska-add-q1.txt). Oak asks "Which subtraction equation
  could this bar model could represent?" with the answers "8 - 5 = 3" and "8 - 3 = 5"
  (thenational.academy-res-addition-lessons-understand-the-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt, Starter
  quiz Q5).
- Order: the place of the unknown picks the operation. "students should see that the same model can represent both
  addition and subtraction (part-whole) situations, and the operation needed depends on the position of the unknown."
  (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt, About this Lesson).
- Order: a family from two numbers. "give students two numbers (i.e. 7 and 4) and ask them to find a third number that
  will form a fact family; here the student might add either 3 or 11."
  (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt, IM Commentary).
- Examples: "There are 5 white butterflies and 3 brown butterflies. If you put them all together, how many are there?"
  (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt, Introduction). "Sam had 10 red
  marbles and 12 blue marbles. How many marbles did he have altogether?" and "I had 10 pencils and I gave 6 away, how

  (thenational.academy-rs-0-to-5-lessons-use-a-bar-model-to-represent-a-whole-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt, Exit quiz

- Examples: a question mark marks the unknown. "A question mark is used to indicate the part that is unknown."
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-ca-the-bar-model.txt). The Japanese board writes "all together ?" over the strip of
  "5 white butterflies" and "3 brown butterflies"
  (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt, board plan).
- Words: "bar model" in England. "Bar model - A bar model is a pictorial representation of a problem where bars are
  used to represent the known and unknown quantities."
  (thenational.academy-rs-0-to-5-lessons-use-a-bar-model-to-represent-a-whole-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt,
  Keywords). "tape diagram" in the US and the Japanese lessons (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page
  16, lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt). "Part-Whole Model" and
  "model drawing" in Singapore
  (isomer-user-content.by.gov.sg-0Drawing-20for-20Young-20Learners-Mathematics-Parents-20Workshop-202026.txt). The
  labels in a Singapore bar are "whole", "part", "Total", "Gave" and "Left" (same file, Qn 1).
- Words: Oak's sentences for a bar. "There are ___ objects. ___ is the whole. There are ___ and ___. ___ is a part. ___

  Key learning points).
- Errors: the parts drawn equal. "Children may not see the bar model as proportional, so may think that the parts are
  equal." The advice: "Encourage children to see that the bars are proportional to help them get a sense of the size

  able to see that the sizes of the quantities in the problem can be shown by using length (paper strips)?"

- Errors: the bar taken as a method. "It is not a method for solving problems, but a way of revealing the mathematical

## Against the brief

- The word. "fact families" is the IM word, given with its reason: "these are often known as "fact families""
  (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt). IM's own course writes "related facts"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Units 3 and 8),
  Eureka "related addition and subtraction facts" (onlinemathlearning.com-relate-addition-subtraction-facts.txt),
  England "related subtraction facts" and "the inverse relationship" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, pages 7 and
  12), the US standard and Japan "the relationship between addition and subtraction"
  (.cache/curricula/us-common-core-math.pdf, page 15, .cache/curricula/japan-course-of-study-2008-en.pdf, page 9), and
  Bremen "Umkehraufgaben" (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 40). Oak and NCETM give "inverse" in
  Year 1 with the plain word "undo": "Addition can 'undo' subtraction."
  (thenational.academy-and-subtraction-lessons-know-that-addition-and-subtraction-are-inverse-operations.txt).
- Four facts or eight. The brief gives a family four facts. IM notes "there are actually 8 equations that can be
  written for every fact family" (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt), the
  progression "Each addition and subtraction equation has seven related equations." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf,
  page 18), and England writes "7 = 16 – 9" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, page 7). The other four put the whole
  or the answer before the = sign.
- The swap of the parts. Section 010 writes both orders of the sum, such as 9 + 4 = 13 and 4 + 9 = 13. NCETM teaches
  "addends can be represented in any order" as outcome 2 of the same unit, before subtraction
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-cp-year-1-unit-6-additive-structures.txt), and the US standard sets "If 8 + 3 = 11
  is known, then 3 + 8 = 11 is also known." in the same cluster (.cache/curricula/us-common-core-math.pdf, page 15,
  1.OA.B.3). The book's story `any-order`, which teaches the order of the numbers, needs `times-tables` and comes later
  (specs/stories/any-order.md). The brief's Needs leave the swap out.
- The order of 020 and 030. NCETM teaches the missing part, outcomes 15 and 16, before the inverse, outcomes 17 and 18
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-cp-year-1-unit-6-additive-structures.txt), and NCETM 1.5 has the missing addend
  in its teaching points (ncetm.org.uk-oom-resources-primm-105-additive-structures-introduction-to-aggregation-and-partitioning.txt).
  The progression treats them as two separate ideas: "in a different way than as reversing the action, namely as finding
  an unknown addend." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf, page 13). The brief has undoing in 020 before
  the missing part in 030.
- Checking a sum by taking away. Section 020 checks a sum with a subtraction. England puts checking in Year 2, "use
  this to check calculations", with the example "adding to check subtraction" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf,
  page 12), and Bremen at the end of year 4 (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 40).
- The number line in 020. None of the read sources teaches undoing first on a number line. Oak goes from acting out to
  pictures to equations, with one number line question in a quiz
  (thenational.academy-ction-lessons-represent-the-inverse-relationship-between-addition-and-subtraction.txt), and
  Nuffield finds objects work better than numbers alone: "they used the inversion principle much less often in this
  task than in the task with bricks" (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 24). The brief's
  counters first fits this.
- The size of the numbers. The brief uses wholes from 11 to 20 from its second page, such as 13, 14 and 16. IM keeps
  the first missing parts within 10, "The numbers are kept within 10"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2), and gives
  the family task "after the students have had quite a bit of experience adding and subtracting within 10"
  (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt). Oak's missing parts sit in its units on
  0 to 5 and 6 to 10. England Year 1 has "related subtraction facts within 20" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf,
  page 7).
- The bar's first page. The brief turns counters into a bar on one page. The sources take steps: objects in a line,
  then linking cubes with a bar drawn beside them and "what’s the same, what’s different?"
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-ca-the-bar-model.txt), blocks boxed in with lines, then a paper strip cut to the
  length of each part (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt), and
  individual boxes before "one long rectangle/bar with a number" (mathsnoproblem.com-en-approach-bar-modelling.txt).
- When the bar comes. England and Japan bring in the bar or tape in Year 1 or grade 1
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-ca-the-bar-model.txt, lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt),
  Oak in its unit on 0 to 5, before any subtraction
  (thenational.academy-rs-0-to-5-lessons-use-a-bar-model-to-represent-a-whole-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt). The US
  progression puts the tape diagram "Later in Grade 1" and first for comparing (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.pdf,
  page 12). The brief keeps the bar for the last section, after the missing part, and for a whole up to 20.
- Drawing the bar. The brief's reader sets the bar by dragging its end and split, and the exercises ask the reader to
  build the bar for a fact. The Japanese plan uses the bar given and does not ask children to draw it: "not on
  requiring them to draw the diagrams" (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt).
- The bar's lengths. The brief draws parts as lengths. Oak warns children may think "the parts are equal" and wants
  the bars proportional, without being exact at first: "it is not crucial the bars are accurate at this stage."
  (thenational.academy-rs-0-to-5-lessons-use-a-bar-model-to-represent-a-whole-partitioned-into-two-parts.txt).
- Situations. None of the read sources uses a bar of chocolate, a box of eggs, a domino or a squad and bench. The
  sources use a whole of one kind of thing in two sorts: presents with and without bows, balloons, a fruit bowl,
  children standing and sitting, fiction and other books (ncetm.org.uk-media-yoyphd14-pska-add-q1.txt), red and blue
  sheets of paper, white and brown butterflies (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt),
  men and women at a concert (isomer-user-content.by.gov.sg-0Drawing-20for-20Young-20Learners-Mathematics-Parents-20Workshop-202026.txt),
  red and blue marbles, red and yellow pencils (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-ca-the-bar-model.txt), and two colour
  counters (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt). For a
  hidden part they use counters under a cup or in a hand, pennies in a second pocket, claps or hops still to do and a
  ladybird still to climb (IM scope file, Unit 2, and the three Oak files). For undoing they use a shirt dirtied and
  washed, a table set and cleared, sweets given and eaten (nuffieldfoundation.org-sites-default-files-P2.pdf, page 24).
- Section 040's second challenge, a family that holds two drawn numbers, is the IM extension "give students two
  numbers (i.e. 7 and 4) and ask them to find a third number that will form a fact family; here the student might add
  either 3 or 11." (tasks.illustrativemathematics.org-content-standards-OA-1-B-tasks-1214.txt). The source fits the
  brief and offers two answers per pair, the sum and the difference.
- Covering a part. The brief's domino page and end page cover a part. IM's "How many counters are under the cup?"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2) and the
  Japanese "square tile to cover up the quantities" (lessonresearch.net-wp-content-uploads-2018-08-Solving-problems-using-diagrams.txt)
  do the same, so the sources support it.
