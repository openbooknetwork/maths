# A subtraction can ask for a missing part

## Header
- Assumes: from section `four-facts`, the reader knows that a whole and its two parts give a fact family of four
  facts, two additions and two subtractions, written under the frames, that a tap on a fact lights the counters it
  speaks of, a subtraction drawing the part it takes away as dashed rings, and that a subtraction takes a known part
  away from the whole to find a covered part, as 7 − 4 = 3 on the domino (page 120). From `number-bonds` the reader
  knows the whole, the known part and the missing part, and finding the missing part by counting on from the known
  part to the whole (page 120). From `adding-within-20` the reader knows the two ten frames with red and blue
  counters, the number sentence and its reading, and the difference found by counting on from the smaller number to
  the greater (page 430).
- Teaches: a subtraction such as 13 − 8 asks for the missing part of a whole of 13 with a known part of 8, which is
  how many to add to 8 to make 13, so counting on from 8 to 13 answers it, and so does the addition 8 + 5 = 13 of
  its family.
- Introduces:
  - Term: `the known part, the missing part (whole and parts)`, in the sense the glossary gives it from
    `number-bonds`, now read in a subtraction: in 13 − 8 the number before the − sign is the whole, the number after
    it is the known part, and the answer is the missing part.
  - Controls: the outlined empty places of the missing part beside the red counters of the known part, and the
    counter a tap puts into the next place, which carries the number the count has reached (210). The open
    subtraction under the frames, such as "7 − 2", which gives way to the four facts of its family once every place
    is filled (210). The stepper on the whole, which empties the places and changes how many there are (210). The
    look `squad`, whose bench seats the next player at each tap, with shirt numbers that count on (610). The Check
    button of a missing part with no places and no numbers on the counters (710).
- Uses:
  - A whole of 7 with a known part of 2 red counters and 5 outlined places. Counting on from 2 fills them with 3, 4,
    5, 6, 7, and the family of 2, 5 and 7 replaces the open 7 − 2: 2 + 5 = 7, 5 + 2 = 7, 7 − 5 = 2 and 7 − 2 = 5.
    The whole steps from 5 to 13, and 9 gives 7 places, 9 − 2 = 7 and 2 + 7 = 9 (210).
  - A squad of 16 players with 11 on the pitch, shirts 1 to 11. Players 12 to 16 sit on the bench until the squad is
    full, and the bench holds 5, so 16 − 11 = 5 (610).
  - A challenge with a whole from 11 to 18 and a known part from 3 to 9, such as 13 − 8 (710).

## Content

### 210 A subtraction asks for the missing part

The idea: 7 − 2 asks "2 and what make 7?" When 7 is the whole and 2 the known part, the subtraction asks how many
you add to the known part to make the whole, which is the missing part. You answer it by counting on from the known
part to the whole, as you found the missing part of a number bond and the difference of two numbers. The text points
back to both in one sentence and does not teach counting on again.

The example is IM's situation, some counters in all and some of them in sight, with 7 counters and 2 in sight. On
the figure the 2 red counters are the known part, and the 5 outlined places after them are where the missing part
goes. Counting on from 2: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. Five numbers are said, so five counters go in, and the count was the addition
2 + 5 = 7. It answers 7 − 2: 7 − 2 = 5. Once the places are full, the four facts of the family of 2, 5 and 7 take the
place of the open subtraction, with 2 + 5 = 7 and 7 − 2 = 5 among them. Both readings give 5: subtracting 2 from 7
leaves 5, and 2 and 5 make 7. The answer 5 is neither the first number said, 3, nor the last, 7, so the reader sees
that the answer is how many numbers are said.

The new case: the whole steps to 9. Seven places show, and 9 − 2 asks "2 and what make 9?" Counting on 3, 4, 5, 6, 7,
8, 9 puts in seven counters, so 2 + 7 = 9 and 9 − 2 = 7.

One sentence names the error Oak warns of: adding the two numbers you know, 7 + 2 = 9, gives more than the whole of
7, so it cannot be a part of it.

### 610 The players on the bench (world page)

A coach picks a squad of players for a match. 11 of them start on the pitch and the rest wait on the bench to come
on later. The coach knows the squad and the players on the pitch, and finds how many are on the bench with a
subtraction. The squad is the whole, the players on the pitch are the known part, and the bench is the missing part.

The example is a squad of 14 with 11 on the pitch, so the figure's squad of 16 is left for the reader. 14 − 11 asks
"11 and what make 14?" Counting on from 11: 12, 13, 14. Three players sit on the bench, so 14 − 11 = 3, and
11 + 3 = 14. The reader works out the figure's bench before tapping. On the figure each player on the pitch is
a red counter of the known part, wearing shirts 1 to 11. Each player who sits on the bench wears the next shirt
number, which is the number the count reaches, and the players stop coming once the squad of 16 is full. The reader
counts on as the players sit, then counts the players on the bench. The count on from 11 is short because the known
part is the greater of the two parts.

### 710 Put in the missing part (challenge)

The goal and the idea are under "Challenge" below. The text says the goal, works one example in numbers, 13 − 8
asks "8 and what make 13?", and says how to put counters in and take them out and when to press Check.

## Interactivity

### 210
- Shows: the two ten frames with 2 round red counters in the first cells of the first frame and 5 outlined empty
  places, in the shape of a square counter, in cells 3 to 7. Cells 8 to 10 and the second frame are plain empty
  cells. Under the frames the open subtraction "7 − 2". A stepper "Whole" from 5 to 13 at 7, and the readout of the
  counters put in, under the name the engine author settles, such as "Counters put in", at 0.
- The reader taps a place or anywhere on the frames, and a square blue counter fills the next place carrying the
  number the count reaches, 3 for the first. A tap on a counter put in takes the last one out. Once the fifth
  counter, carrying 7, is in, the open subtraction gives way to the four facts of the family of 2, 5 and 7. A tap on
  a fact picks it as on the pages of `four-facts`: 7 − 2 = 5 draws the 2 red counters as dashed rings and lights the
  5 blue ones. A step of the whole empties the places, draws as many places as the new whole minus 2, and writes the
  new open subtraction, such as "9 − 2". The whole runs from 5 to 13, so the missing part runs from 3 to 11 and is
  never equal to the known part, and the family always has four facts.
- In general: tap the frames, and a counter fills the next empty place and shows the number the count has reached.
  When the places are full, the open subtraction gives way to the four facts of its family. Step the whole, and the
  places empty and grow or shrink for a new subtraction.

### 610
- Shows: a football pitch from above with 11 players in red shirts numbered 1 to 11, and beside it an empty bench
  with no outlined seats. Under the figure the open subtraction "16 − 11". The readouts "Squad" 16, "On the pitch" 11
  and "On the bench" 0.
- The reader taps the bench, and a player in the next shirt number, 12 first, sits on it. A tap on a seated player
  takes the last one off. Once player 16 sits, the squad is full, a tap seats no one more, and the line reads
  "16 − 11 = 5".
- In general: tap the bench, and the next player sits on it in a shirt that counts on, until the squad is full. The
  reader counts on as players sit, then counts the bench.

### 710
- Shows: the frames with the known part as red counters and no places, the goal line naming the subtraction, Check
  and "Another one". See "Challenge".
- The reader taps an empty cell, and a blue counter goes in the next cell. A tap on a blue counter takes the last one
  out. Then the reader presses Check.
- In general: put in the counters a subtraction asks for, then commit the answer.

## Challenge

- Goal: put in the missing part that a subtraction asks for, then press Check. The goal line names the subtraction:
  "Put in the part that 13 − 8 asks for, then press Check." The open subtraction stands under the frames as well.
- Drawn each time: a whole from 11 to 18 and a known part from 3 to 9, below the whole, as the goal `missing-part`
  of the brief draws them. The frames open with the known part as red counters and no blue counter, so the missing
  part, at least 2, is not there as the page opens. "Another one" draws again and empties the frames.
- Reached: a tap on an empty cell puts a blue counter in the next cell, and a tap on a blue counter takes the last
  one out. The goal holds when the blue counters are the whole minus the known part, and the reader commits with
  Check. A wrong Check says what the two groups make, in the reader's numbers: "8 and 6 make 14, more than 13." or
  "8 and 4 make 12, less than 13." The hint: "13 − 8 asks: 8 and what make 13? Count on from 8 to 13 and put in a
  counter for each number you say." Once the goal holds the four facts of the family show under the frames, and a
  change of the counters takes them away again.
- Hidden: the outlined places, the numbers on the counters, every readout and every link, and the four facts until
  the goal holds. The empty cells give nothing away, since the two frames have 20 cells whatever the whole.
- The idea used: a subtraction asks for the missing part of its whole, found by counting on from the known part or
  by an addition of its family. The challenge needs this one idea, as a first challenge does.
