## Content

### 410 A row of counters becomes a bar

The idea: a bar model draws a whole and its two parts as lengths. Each counter of a row gets the same length of bar,
so a part is as long as its number of counters, and the whole bar is as long as both parts together. The parts are
as long as each other only when their numbers are equal.

The example is NCETM's marbles with numbers within 10. There are 5 red marbles and 3 blue marbles, lined up in a row.
On the figure each round red counter stands for a red marble and each square blue counter for a blue marble. Under
the row lies a bar of the same length. The first part of the bar, in red, lies under the 5 red counters, and the
second part, in blue, under the 3 blue counters. The 5 stands in the first part, the 3 in the second, and the whole's
number 8 above the bar. In Oak's words: there are 8 marbles, 8 is the whole, 5 is a part and 3 is a part. Under the
bar the line reads 5 + 3 = 8.

The text asks NCETM's question: what is the same and what is different? The counters and the bar have the same
length and the same parts. The bar has no gaps, and its numbers stand in it, so it needs no counting.

The text names the end of the bar and asks the reader to drag it to 10. Two blue counters join the row, the second
part grows to 5, and the line reads 5 + 5 = 10. The two parts are now as long as each other, because 5 = 5. Dragged
back to 6, the second part holds 1 counter and is the shorter part, and the line reads 5 + 1 = 6. The end runs from
6 to 12, so the red part stays the 5 red marbles and the second part holds from 1 to 7 blue ones.

### 420 One bar model holds a fact family

The idea: one bar model holds the four facts of its family. The two parts together make the whole, in either order,
and taking either part away from the whole leaves the other part. NCETM gives the bar's four relationships as
a = b + c, a = c + b, a − b = c and a − c = b, which this story writes with the whole after the = sign.

The example is the bar of page 410 without its counters: 8 split into 5 and 3. Under it stand the four facts
5 + 3 = 8, 3 + 5 = 8, 8 − 3 = 5 and 8 − 5 = 3. Oak asks which subtraction a bar of 8, 5 and 3 shows, and both
8 − 5 = 3 and 8 − 3 = 5 are right.

Each fact speaks of the bar. 5 + 3 = 8 puts the two parts together into the whole bar. 8 − 3 = 5 starts from the whole
bar and takes the part of 3 away, and the part of 5 is left. 8 − 5 = 3 takes the part of 5 away, and the part of 3 is
left. The text tells the reader to tap a subtraction and watch the part it takes away go pale. So a bar shows taking
away as a length taken off the whole, where the counters of section `four-facts` showed it as counters taken away.

### 430 Move the split, keep the whole

The idea: the split is the line across the bar where its two parts meet. Moving the split changes the two parts and
keeps the whole, because the end of the bar stays where it is. What one part gains, the other part loses. Each place
of the split gives a new family with the same whole.

The example is a bar of 11 split at 7, the family of 7, 4 and 11, with its four facts 7 + 4 = 11, 4 + 7 = 11,
11 − 4 = 7 and 11 − 7 = 4 under it. The reader drags the split one step to the left. The first part becomes 6 and the
second part 5, and the bar still has 11 above it. The four facts become 6 + 5 = 11, 5 + 6 = 11, 11 − 5 = 6 and
11 − 6 = 5, the family of 6, 5 and 11. The first part lost 1 and the second part gained 1.

The new case is the split dragged to 4. The parts are 4 and 7, and the family is 7, 4 and 11 again, with its parts the
other way round, as the parts of `number-bonds` swap places and keep the whole. So the 10 places of the split give 5
families of 11: 1 and 10, 2 and 9, 3 and 8, 4 and 7, and 5 and 6. A bar of 11 has no split with two equal parts,
since 11 is odd, and the text leaves that out.

### 440 A question mark marks the missing part

The idea: a bar model shows the numbers of a story. A story gives the whole and one part, and the bar puts a question
mark on the number the story asks for. Where the question mark stands picks the calculation. A missing part is the
whole minus the known part, as section `missing-part` taught, and a missing whole is the two parts added.

The example is NCETM's take away, with numbers within 20: "You have 15 pencils and give 6 away. How many do you have
now?" On the figure the whole bar is the 15 pencils you had, the red first part is the 6 you gave away, and the blue
second part is the pencils you have now. The story does not give that number, so the second part carries a question
mark. 15 is the whole and 6 is the known part, so the line under the bar reads 15 − 6, open. The reader taps the
card to lift it. The second part reads 9, and the line reads 15 − 6 = 9. You have 9 pencils now.

The new case is the same bar with the whole covered: "You gave 6 pencils away and have 9 left. How many did you have?"
The reader taps the 15, the card covers it, and the line reads 6 + 9, open. Both parts are known, so adding them
finds the whole, and the lifted card gives 6 + 9 = 15. This is the Japanese lesson's tile, put on each number in turn.

### 620 Break a bar of chocolate

The world page. A bar of chocolate has 12 squares in a row. You break off 5 squares for a friend and keep the rest.
The bar is the whole, 12 squares, and the two pieces are its parts. The first piece, on the left, is the friend's,
and the second piece is yours. The text says "piece" for the chocolate and "part" for the bar model it stands for.

The bar opens broken at 3, with 3 squares in the first piece and 9 in the second, and the four facts of 3, 9 and 12
under it. Before the reader breaks the bar again, the text asks the question of the page: how many squares stay with
you, and which subtraction finds it? It points to the idea and leaves the numbers to the reader: 12 is the whole and
the friend's 5 is the known part. Then the reader drags the split to 5, and the second piece shows the number of
squares you keep. The four facts under the bar change to the family of 5 and 12. The text asks which of the four
facts answers the question, and which fact says that the two pieces make the whole bar again.

Answer, for the writer and the engine author and kept out of the text: you keep 7 squares. 12 − 5 = 7 answers the
question, and 5 + 7 = 12 says the two pieces make the bar. The family is 5 + 7 = 12, 7 + 5 = 12, 12 − 7 = 5 and
12 − 5 = 7.

### 720 Set the bar model of a fact

The first challenge. The page draws one fact, such as 16 − 7 = 9, and writes it under a bar of 10 split at 5. The
reader drags the end to the fact's whole and the split to one of its parts, then presses the Check button.

The text says how. The three numbers of a fact are a whole and its two parts. In an addition the two parts stand
before the = sign and the whole after it. In a subtraction the whole stands first, before the − sign. It gives one
example of each, 12 + 7 = 19 and 19 − 7 = 12, both with the whole 19 and the parts 12 and 7, and says the parts may
lie in either order on the bar. The page draws a whole from 6 to 18, so the text's whole of 19 is not a draw. Once
the goal holds, the rest of the family joins the drawn fact. The button Another one draws a new fact.

### 730 Find a family that holds two numbers

The second challenge. The page draws two different numbers, such as 7 and 4, and the reader finds a third number
that makes a family with them. The reader first decides how to use the two numbers, as the two parts or as the whole
and a part, with the "Use them as" switch. The bar puts the two numbers where that choice says and marks the third
with a question mark. As the two parts, the two numbers stand to scale under a dashed bracket with a question mark
for the whole. As the whole and a part, the bar is a sketch: the known part and a dashed, unfilled part with a
question mark take half of the bar each, under the whole's bracket. The reader works the third number out, sets it
on the stepper "Third number" and presses the Check button.

The text says how, and gives no answer of a draw. A family has three numbers, a whole and two parts. Two of them are
given, and the reader decides which they are. Used as the two parts, the third number is their whole, the two parts
added. Used as the whole and a part, the greater number is the whole and the less a part, and the third number is
the missing part, the whole minus the known part, as section `missing-part` taught. Both uses make a family, and both
count. The text works one example, 15 and 4, which the page does not draw since its numbers run from 2 to 12: as the
two parts the third number is 19, and with 15 as the whole and 4 as a part it is 11. The text does not work the goal
line's 7 and 4. Check takes the sum or the difference whichever use the switch shows, and moves the switch to the
use that matches. Once the goal holds, the family is drawn to scale, its four facts show, and the done line names
the other family as well.

### 810 What comes after fact families

The end page. The figure shows a bar of 14 split at 8, with a question mark on the second part and the line 14 − 8
under it. The text sums the story up in the order it was taught. A whole and its two parts give four facts, two
additions and two subtractions, called a fact family. A subtraction can ask for a missing part, and counting on from
the known part to the whole finds it. Subtracting a number undoes adding it, so one fact checks another. A bar model
holds the whole family, and a question mark marks the number to find.

The page then names the stories that follow. `missing-number` puts a box where this bar has its question mark and
writes the question as 8 + ▢ = 14, and the family of 8 and 14 finds the box, since 14 − 8 = 6. `column-methods` adds
and subtracts numbers up to the thousands in columns, and checks a subtraction with an addition of its family.

Example: 14 − 8 = 6, the missing part of a bar of 14 with the known part 8.
