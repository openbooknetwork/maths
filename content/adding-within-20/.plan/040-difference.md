# The difference

## Header
- Assumes: the reader writes and reads a subtraction such as 9 − 4 = 5 as what is left after counters are taken
  away, from section `taking-away`. From section `counting-on` the reader knows the number line from 0 to 20, its
  marks, the point, a jump as an arc to the next mark, the start, which is not counted, the buttons Jump left and Jump
  right, and counting back for 1, 2 or 3. From the story `more-and-less` the reader knows pair lines, the ring on a
  counter without a partner, the tags more, fewer and the same, and the difference of two groups as the number of
  counters left over.
- Teaches: the difference between two numbers is written as a subtraction, the greater number minus the less,
  because taking the paired counters away from the greater row leaves the counters left over, so one written line
  can say what is left or say a difference, and counting on from the less number to the greater finds it by counting
  up, which is surer than counting back a long way.
- Introduces:
  - Term: `difference (numbers)`, how much greater one number is than another, written as the greater minus the
    less, as 13 − 8 = 5.
  - Controls: the written line of a difference under two rows, which puts the greater row's number first and turns
    round when the other row passes it (410). The written line of a difference under the number line, which reads
    "13 − 8" until the point reaches the second point and then adds "= 5" (430). The square second point with its
    flag, which stays to the right of the start, first used in this story on page 430.
- Uses:
  - Two rows of 11 and 7 counters, each on a stepper from 1 to 12, paired by the scene, with 7 pairs, 4 counters left
    over and the written line 11 − 7 = 4 (410). Taking the 7 paired counters of the top row away from its 11 leaves
    the 4 left over. Adding 11 and 7 would count all 18 counters. The bottom row stepped from 7 up to 12 writes
    11 − 8 = 3 and on to 11 − 11 = 0, then turns to 12 − 11 = 1.
  - Two questions in words with the numbers of 410: 11 apples in a bowl with 7 eaten, and 11 apples beside 7 pears.
  - The number line from 0 to 20 with the start at 8 and the second point at 13. Five jumps to the right count up 9,
    10, 11, 12, 13 and write 13 − 8 = 5. The text sets it beside counting back 8 from 13. Then the second point moves
    to 15, and two more jumps write 15 − 8 = 7 (430).

## Content

### 410 Write the difference as a subtraction

The page goes over pairing two rows, the one page of the story that does, and then gives the reason the difference
is a subtraction, on the same figure.

The top row has 11 counters and the bottom row 7. Pair lines join each counter of the bottom row to a counter of the
top row, and the 4 top counters left over carry a ring because they have no partner. The top row has 4 more and the
bottom row has 4 fewer, and 4 is the difference between the two rows. That is the logic of `more-and-less`, in three
sentences with no new term.

The reason comes from the figure. The 11 counters of the top row are 7 counters with a partner and 4 without. The 7
with a partner match the 7 counters of the bottom row, one each. Take those 7 counters away from the 11, and the 4
without a partner are left. So the difference of 11 and 7 is written as the subtraction 11 − 7 = 4, read "11 minus 7
is equal to 4". The difference between two numbers is how much greater one is than the other.

The same written line says what is left and says a difference. "There are 11 apples in a bowl. 7 are eaten. How many
are left?" takes 7 apples away from 11. "There are 11 apples and 7 pears. How many more apples are there than
pears?" compares 11 with 7. Both questions have the written line 11 − 7 = 4.

The question "how many more" has the word "more" in it, and its answer is a subtraction. Adding 11 and 7 would count
every counter on the figure, 18. The question asks only for the 4 ringed counters, which are part of the top row, and
finding them takes the 7 paired counters away.

The greater number comes first because the paired counters are taken from the row with more. The page asks the reader
to step the bottom row up one counter at a time. The line reads 11 − 8 = 3, 11 − 9 = 2, 11 − 10 = 1, and 11 − 11 = 0
when the rows are the same and their difference is 0. One more step makes the bottom row the row with more: the ring
moves to its last counter, and the written line turns round to 12 − 11 = 1, with the blue 12 first and the red 11
after the sign. The 11 counters of the top row do not hold 12 counters to take away, so the line starts from the
row with more, whichever row that is.

### 430 Count on from the less number to the greater

13 − 8 is the difference between 13 and 8. The page finds it on the number line by counting on from the less number.
Start on 8, the number after the sign −, which is the start. The start is not counted. Each jump to the right says
the next number, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, and the fifth jump lands on the square second point at 13, so 13 − 8 = 5.

Read as a take away, 13 − 8 would count back 8 from 13: 12, 11, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5. Saying the numbers down is
harder than saying them up, and a count back of 8 is a long way to keep track of. That is why section `counting-on`
counts back only 1, 2 or 3. Counting on says the numbers in the order of the count, from the less number up to the
greater, and it works for the line 13 − 8 whichever question it answers. To work out a subtraction this way, start on
the number after the sign − and count the jumps on to the number before it.

The page ends on a new case. The reader drags the square second point to 15 and counts on again from where the point
stands. Two more jumps land on 15, and the line reads 15 − 8 = 7. The answer 7 is the number of jumps from the start.
The second point stays to the right of the start, so the jumps always run up from the less number.

## Interactivity

### 410
- Shows: a top row of 11 red counters above a bottom row of 7 blue counters. A pair line joins each bottom counter to
  the top counter above it, and the 4 top counters left over carry a ring. The tag at the end of the top row reads
  "more" and the tag of the bottom row reads "fewer". Under the rows stands the written line 11 − 7 = 4, with 11 in
  red, 7 in blue, and the sign and the answer in ink. The panel holds the steppers "Top row" and "Bottom row", each
  from 1 to 12, and the readouts "Pairs" at 7, "Without a partner" at 4 and "In words" at "11 minus 7 is equal to 4".
  The link `pairs` in the text lights the 7 pair lines and the counters at both ends of each, so the 7 paired top
  counters show beside the 4 ringed ones.
- The reader: steps either row up or down, and the text leads the bottom row up from 7 to 12.
- The figure: adds or takes the last counter of that row, draws the pairs again and rings the counters left over.
  The tags follow, as in `more-and-less`. The written line writes the greater row's number, minus the less row's
  number, equal to the counters left over. As the bottom row grows from 7 to 11 the line counts down to 11 − 11 = 0
  and both tags read "the same". At 12 the ring moves to the bottom row and the line turns round to 12 − 11 = 1, with
  the blue 12 first.
- Action: change the size of a row, and the written line writes the row with more first and the counters left over
  as its answer, turning round when the other row passes it.

### 430
- Shows: the number line from 0 to 20 in ones, every mark labelled. The start is a ring round the mark of 8 with the
  red round point inside it, and the point's flag reads 8. The blue square second point stands at 13 with its flag.
  Under the line the written line reads 13 − 8, with 13 in blue and 8 in red. The panel holds the buttons Jump left
  and Jump right and the readout "Jumps" at 0.
- The reader: presses Jump right until the point lands on 13. Then drags the square second point to 15 and presses
  Jump right again.
- The figure: each press of Jump right draws an arc above the line from the point's mark to the next and moves the
  point there. "Jumps" counts the arcs. When the point stands on the second point, the written line adds = 5 and
  reads 13 − 8 = 5. A jump past it takes the answer off again, and Jump left takes the last arc back. A drag of the
  second point rewrites the line with its number, 15 − 8, which gains = 7 when the point lands on 15. The second point
  stops one mark to the right of the start, at 9 here, and goes no further left. A drag of the start carries the
  point and its arcs along, as in section `counting-on`, and stops one mark to the left of the second point.
- Action: jump from the less number up to the greater, and the written line gains its answer when the point lands on
  the greater.

## Contract

The section uses `written: difference` of `counters/match` with its links `written` and `reading`, and `start` and
`written: difference` of `line/line`. It no longer uses `counters/add`. Two changes to the engine, each marked `new`:

| Change | Scene | What it draws and does |
|---|---|---|
| The link `pairs` lights the counters of each pair `new` | `counters/match` | The link `pairs` already reads the number of pairs and lights the pair lines. It also lights the two counters at the ends of each pair line, so the text can point at the 7 paired counters of the top row beside the 4 ringed ones. Every page of `match` that links `pairs` gets it. |
| The second point stays to the right of the start `new` | `line/line` | On a page with `start` and `written: difference`, a drag or an arrow key of the second point stops on the mark one to the right of the start, and a drag of the start stops on the mark one to the left of the second point, beside its stop at the end of the line. `kitap check` fails such a page whose `second` is at or left of its `start`, with a message that asks for a second point greater than the start. The written line then always reads the second point minus the start, and the jumps run to the right. |

### 410 `write-the-difference-as-a-subtraction`

Title: "Write the difference as a subtraction"

```yaml
hesap:
  scene: counters/match
  top: { stepper: [1, 12], start: 11 }
  bottom: { stepper: [1, 12], start: 7 }
  pair: auto
  word: true
  written: difference
  readout: [pairs, unpaired, reading]
```

Links: `top` starts at 11, `bottom` at 7, `pairs` at 7, `unpaired` at 4, `top-word` at "more", `bottom-word` at
"fewer", `written` at "11 − 7 = 4" and `reading` at "11 minus 7 is equal to 4". The readout labels are "Pairs",
"Without a partner" and "In words". The rows reach 12, the scene's `ROW_MAX`, with a tag after each, and the counters
stay large enough to tap on a 390px phone.

## Sources

The approach:

- Matching two groups comes before a written difference. "Matching with objects and with drawings, and labeling each
  quantity (e.g., J or Julie and L or Lucy) is helpful. Later in Grade 1, a tape diagram can be used."
  (`content/adding-within-20/.references/isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt`, page 12). Page 410 starts
  from the paired rows of `more-and-less` and writes the line under them.
- The difference builds on saying which group has more. "Compare problems build upon Kindergarten comparisons, in
  which students identified ”Which is more?” or “Which is less?” without ascertaining the difference between the
  numbers." (same file, page 12). The more-and-less pages 230 "Count the counters left over" and 240 "How many more,
  and how many fewer?" (`content/more-and-less/pages/`) taught the number left over as the difference of two rows,
  which page 410 goes over in its first paragraph.
- The difference is not in the figure as objects. "in Compare problems, one of the quantities (the difference) is
  not present in the situation physically" (same file, page 12). Page 410 makes it present: the 7 paired counters of
  the greater row are lit, and taking them away leaves the 4 ringed counters, which is the take away the reader knows
  from section `taking-away`.
- One written line for two meanings. IM: "Students analyze both addition (\(3+7=10\)) and subtraction (\(10-3=7\))
  equations that can be used to represent the same problem."
  (`content/adding-within-20/.references/curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt`,
  Unit 2, Section C). The brief's Teaches puts what is left and the difference on one line, which page 410 now says
  with its own rows.
- The apples. "‘There are eleven apples in a bowl. We eat seven. How many are left?’ (reduction)"
  (`content/adding-within-20/.references/ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt`, page 24,
  step 6:7). Its numbers are the rows of 410, so the page asks it beside a question of how many more apples than
  pears.
- "More" read as adding. "In the question, ‘How many more?’, children may focus on the word ‘more’, believing they
  need to do an addition calculation instead of finding the difference." and "adding together the values in the
  calculation" (`content/adding-within-20/.references/ncetm.org.uk-media-jqmjvenm-pska-add-q3.txt`, Common errors).
  NCETM's remedy is a picture of the two amounts one under the other. Page 410 uses the rows for it: the sum would
  count all 18 counters, and the question points at the 4 ringed ones.
- The greater number first. "knowing that the difference between 4 and 6 is 2, but recording this as 4 - 6 = 2" and
  "the whole (/ the larger number of those being compared) has to be written first in the expression."
  (`content/adding-within-20/.references/thenational.academy-es-maths-primary-ks1-units-calculating-within-20-lessons-calculate-the-difference.txt`,
  Common misconception). Page 410 leads the bottom row past the top row step by step, so the reader sees the line
  turn round, and the reason is the take away: the paired counters come out of the row with more.
- Counting on from the less number finds the difference. "Starting at 12, children will count on 3 to reach 15"
  (`ncetm.org.uk-media-jqmjvenm-pska-add-q3.txt`, Subtraction as difference). Oxford: "we start with the number to be
  taken away (usually the smaller number) and then see how many we need to add on, or count on, to reach the bigger
  number." (`content/adding-within-20/.references/cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt`,
  Counting on). IM: "They may start at 3 and count up to 10 or start at 10 and count back to 3." (IM file above,
  Unit 2, Section C).
- Counting on is surer because of its direction. "Note: Many children attempt to count down for subtraction, but
  counting down is difficult and error-prone. Children are much more successful with counting on; it makes
  subtraction as easy as addition." (`isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt`, page 36, Appendix), and
  "Counting on for subtraction is easier than counting down." (same file, page 15). Page 430 gives this reason and
  no count of jumps as a reason, since 20 − 8 takes 12 jumps on against 8 back. Section `counting-on` keeps counting
  back to 1, 2 or 3 for the same reason, as `decisions.md` records.
- The start is not counted. "Make sure that children are counting the jumps they make, and not the starting point."
  (`content/adding-within-20/.references/nrich.maths.org-5652.txt`, Possible support). Page 430 says it again for
  the start at 8.
