# Research for Adding and subtracting within 20

The files named below sit in `content/adding-within-20/.references/`, except the curricula, which sit in
`.cache/curricula/`. Page numbers of PDFs are the pages of the PDF. The IM file writes its equations in TeX, as in
`\(12+5\)`, and the quotes keep that form. The text of
`isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt` drops the signs + and − from its equations, so its quotes leave
those out.

What the curricula say, for all sections:

- England Year 1 has the signs, numbers to 20 and zero in one list: "read, write and interpret mathematical
  statements involving addition (+), subtraction (–) and equals (=) signs" and "add and subtract one-digit and
  two-digit numbers to 20, including zero" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, page 7, Year 1, Number – addition
  and subtraction).
- England Year 1 joins counting to adding: "Pupils combine and increase numbers, counting forwards and backwards."
  (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, page 7, Notes and guidance).
- The United States teaches the strategies of this story in grade 1: "Use strategies such as counting on; making ten
  (e.g., 8 + 6 = 8 + 2 + 4 = 10 + 4 = 14); decomposing a number leading to a ten (e.g., 13 – 4 = 13 – 3 – 1 = 10 – 1
  = 9)" (.cache/curricula/us-common-core-math.pdf, page 15, 1.OA.C.6).
- Kindergarten in the United States gives the meanings: "Understand addition as putting together and adding to, and
  understand subtraction as taking apart and taking from." (.cache/curricula/us-common-core-math.pdf, page 11, K.OA.A).
- Japan grade 1: "To investigate the ways to calculate the sum of two 1-digit numbers and the difference, which is
  the inverse" and "To think about the ways to calculate simple cases of sums and differences involving 2-digit
  numbers." (.cache/curricula/japan-course-of-study-2008-en.pdf, page 7, Grade 1, A (2)).
- Bremen, years 1 and 2: "Grundvorstellungen zur Subtraktion als Wegnehmen, Ergänzen, Vergleichen"
  (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 21, Addition und Subtraktion).

## 010 joining

- Order: the part and whole come first without signs, then the signs + and = name the joining. NCETM topic 1.5
  follows 1.2 to 1.4 on parts and wholes: "Progress to the use of abstract notation (+, − and =) as a way of
  representing the part–part–whole structure." (ncetm.org.uk-oom-resources-primm-105-additive-structures-introduction-to-aggregation-and-partitioning.txt, Introduction).
- Order: the + sign first, then the = sign, then the sum as a whole. "Teaching point 1: combining two or more parts
  to make a whole is called aggregation; the addition symbol, +, can be used to represent aggregation. Teaching
  point 2: The equals symbol, =, can be used to show equivalence between the whole and the sum of the parts."
  (ncetm.org.uk-oom-resources-primm-105-additive-structures-introduction-to-aggregation-and-partitioning.txt,
  Teaching points).
- Order: the action of adding on follows the joining of two groups as its own topic, 1.6, with a three part story.
  "An addition context described by a ‘first…, then…, now…’ story is an example of augmentation."
  (ncetm.org.uk-sroom-resources-primm-106-additive-structures-introduction-to-augmentation-and-reduction.txt,
  Teaching point 1).
- Order: children join objects and count all before they write equations. "Represent each addend by a group of
  objects. Put the two groups together. Count the total." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 36,
  Appendix, Level 1).
- Order: in kindergarten the teacher writes the line, and children write it in grade 1. "The teacher can write
  expressions (e.g., 3 + 1) to represent operations" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 8,
  Kindergarten). IM grade 1 unit 2: "They work formally with equations for the first time, writing addition and
  subtraction equations that match story problems."
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2, Section A).
- Order: one more and adding zero are their own teaching points. "Teaching point 3: Adding one gives one more;
  subtracting one gives one less." and "Teaching point 7: When zero is added to a number, the number remains
  unchanged" (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-primm-107-addition-and-subtraction-strategies-within-10.txt).
  England: "They should realise the effect of adding or subtracting zero." (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf,
  page 7, Notes and guidance).
- Examples: two kinds of animal joined. "adding the number of two adult cats and four kittens, children are
  encouraged to write an equation to represent how many cats there are altogether"
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-8d8533d695aa6c1-pvltg-ks1-number-addition-subtraction-pt1-1-to-5.txt, Lesson 1).
- Examples: two parts of one group. "there are apples and bananas in a fruit bowl, or children standing up and
  sitting down" (ncetm.org.uk-media-yoyphd14-pska-add-q1.txt, Aggregation (addition) and partitioning (subtraction)
  structures).
- Examples: an action story. "First, there were two children in the book corner. Then, four more children came into
  the book corner. Now, there are six children in the book corner." (ncetm.org.uk-media-pkxnnz0r-pska-add-q2.txt,
  Did you notice that…?).
- Examples: IM uses a shake of two colour counters: "Lesson 6, Activity 1, A Shake and Spill Story Problem"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 3, Section A).
- Words: England Year 1 gives "put together, add, altogether, total" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, page 7,
  Notes and guidance). England gives "sum" in Year 2: "Pupils extend their understanding of the language of addition
  and subtraction to include sum and difference." (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, page 12, Year 2, Notes and
  guidance).
- Words: the progression keeps "sum" for later. "it is better in the earlier grades to use “total” rather than
  “sum.”" because "“Sum” sounds the same as “some,” but has the opposite meaning."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 8, Note on vocabulary).
- Words: Bremen gives "plus, minus, gleich (=)" by the end of year 2 and "Summe, Differenz, Produkt, Quotient,
  addieren, subtrahieren" by the end of year 4: "verwenden Fachbegriffe plus, minus, mal und geteilt korrekt."
  (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 39, Operationsverständnis).
- Words: NCETM uses "sum" and "addend" in Year 1: "Each addend represents a part, and these are combined to form the
  whole/sum" (ncetm.org.uk-oom-resources-primm-105-additive-structures-introduction-to-aggregation-and-partitioning.txt,
  Teaching point 3).
- Errors: the = sign read as "the answer comes next". "They may initially see it as a prompt for the answer to a
  question" (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2).
  NCETM advises lines with an expression on both sides: "the children to see that an equation does not always read
  as finding a solution to a calculation" (ncetm.org.uk-media-8d8533d695aa6c1-pvltg-ks1-number-addition-subtraction-pt1-1-to-5.txt,
  Lesson 4).
- Errors: picking the wrong operation in a new setting. "children not applying their understanding of structures to
  different contexts, which may result in the wrong operation being performed"
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-yoyphd14-pska-add-q1.txt, Common errors).
- Errors: before counting on, a child counts the whole group again. "a student might have to recount entirely a
  collection of known cardinality to which a single object has been added."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 5).

## 020 taking-away

- Order: NCETM teaches taking away in topic 1.6, after joining in 1.5, with the same three part story and the
  signs. "Introduce children to addition as augmentation, and subtraction as reduction (take away), using a
  ‘first…, then…, now…’ story representation and abstract notation (+, − and =)"
  (ncetm.org.uk-sroom-resources-primm-106-additive-structures-introduction-to-augmentation-and-reduction.txt,
  Introduction).
- Order: with objects first. "Represent the total by a group of objects. Take the known addend number of objects
  away. Count the resulting group of objects" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 36, Appendix,
  Level 1).
- Order: zero and the whole number. "Teaching point 7: ... when zero is subtracted from a number, the number remains
  unchanged. Teaching point 8: Subtracting a number from itself gives a difference of zero."
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-primm-107-addition-and-subtraction-strategies-within-10.txt).
- Examples: a ride at a fair. "‘First there were twelve children on the ride. Then four got off. Now there are eight
  children on the ride.’" (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 19, step 6:1).
- Examples: money spent. "Sarah had £6. She spent £2. She now has £4 (reduction)."
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-pkxnnz0r-pska-add-q2.txt, Augmentation (addition) and reduction (subtraction) structures).
- Examples: food eaten. "‘There are eleven apples in a bowl. We eat seven. How many are left?’ (reduction)"
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 24, step 6:7).
- Examples: toys lent. "‘You have 3 toy cars. How many will you have left if you let Charlie play with 1?’"
  (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt, Subtracting in
  everyday life).
- Words: "take away, how many left?, minus, how many more?, less, less than, what’s the difference?" in one list,
  with the warning "This can be confusing at first."
  (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt, Different words for
  subtraction).
- Words: the progression leaves out "minuend" and "subtrahend": "Formal vocabulary for subtraction (“minuend” and
  “subtrahend”) is not needed for Kindergarten, Grade 1, and Grade 2" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt,
  page 8, Note on vocabulary).
- Errors: reading the sign − as "take away". "Common errors in this area may include: children referring to the
  subtraction symbol as ‘take-away’ for all subtraction models." The advice: "it is important that the subtraction
  sign is not referred to as ‘take away’, for example ‘ten, take away four’, as this is not the only structure for
  subtraction." (ncetm.org.uk-media-pkxnnz0r-pska-add-q2.txt, Augmentation and reduction, Common errors).
- Errors: mixing the split of a whole with a take away. "children confusing the structures of partitioning and
  reduction, as they tend to be more familiar with the ‘take away’ structure for subtraction."
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-yoyphd14-pska-add-q1.txt, Common errors).
- Errors: swapping the numbers. "Unlike addition, subtraction calculations cannot be done in any order: 7–4 is not
  the same as 4–7. Children may need help remembering this."
  (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt, Subtraction cannot
  be done in any order).

## 030 counting-on

- What the reader must hold from `counting`: counting on from any number and the meaning of the next number. "Being
  able to count forward, beginning from a given number within the known sequence, is a prerequisite for such counting
  on. Finally, understanding that each successive number name refers to a quantity that is one larger is the
  conceptual start for Grade 1 counting on." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 5, From counting
  to counting on).
- Order: counting all, then counting on. "Count this total but abbreviate the counting by omitting the count of this
  addend; instead, begin with the number word of this addend." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt,
  page 6, Level 2. Counting On).
- Order: small numbers to count on first. US grade 1: "Relate counting to addition and subtraction (e.g., by counting
  on 2 to add 2)." (.cache/curricula/us-common-core-math.pdf, page 15, 1.OA.C.5). IM: "There is an emphasis on adding
  and subtracting 1 or 2 to encourage students to count on or count back"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 1,
  Section A). "All but a few sums within 10 can be found by counting on by 1, 2, or 3, or by making a sum of 10"
  (same file, Unit 3, Section A).
- Order: on a number line, jumps of one before larger jumps. "They can start at a fixed point on a number line and
  will either count on (augmentation) or back (reduction). Initially, they may move in smaller amounts, but as they
  become more confident, they may choose to jump" (ncetm.org.uk-media-pkxnnz0r-pska-add-q2.txt, Augmentation and
  reduction structures).
- Order: the start. Counting on starts from the first number, or from the larger once the order of the numbers is
  free: "One counts on from the first addend (or the larger number is taken as the first addend)."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 37, Appendix, Level 2). "Students might also use the
  commutative property to shorten tasks, by counting on from the larger addend even if it is second" (same file,
  page 14).
- Order: Eureka Math teaches the larger start in lesson 20 of its first module, the module on "The Commutative
  Property of Addition and the Equal Sign": "Circle the larger amount and count on. Write the number sentence
  starting with the larger number." (onlinemathlearning.com-count-on-larger-addend.txt, Lesson 20 Problem Set).
- Order: Oxford says children count back first. "Children are first taught to subtract by counting backwards. In
  this method, we start from the highest number and count backwards."
  (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt, Counting backwards).
- Examples: the count spoken aloud with fingers. "“Niiiiine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen.”" and "Students keep
  track of how many they counted on (here, 4) with fingers, mental images, or physical actions such as head bobs."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 14).
- Examples: 8 − 5 on fingers and 7 − 4 on a line from 0 to 10. "Start from finger 8. Count back 5 fingers. See where
  we land: on finger 3." (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt,
  Counting backwards, Number lines).
- Examples: jumps on a line from 0. "First Leah made a jump of three along her number line and then a jump of four.
  Where did she land?" with a counter on "a 0-10 number line" and the extension "Use a number line which goes to up
  to 20 or 100." (nrich.maths.org-5652.txt, Problem, Teachers' Resources).
- Words: "count on", "count back" (ncetm.org.uk-media-pkxnnz0r-pska-add-q2.txt), "jump" and "secret jump"
  (nrich.maths.org-5652.txt), "hop backwards down the numbers"
  (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt, Blank number lines).
- Errors: counting the start as the first jump. "Make sure that children are counting the jumps they make, and not
  the starting point." (nrich.maths.org-5652.txt, Possible support).
- Errors: counting marks in place of steps. "They may count the starting point 0 and then be off by one, or they
  focus on the spaces and are confused by the location of the numbers at the ends of the spaces." and "lots of
  students just count the hash marks" (oise.utoronto.ca-robertson-blog-power-number-line-model-scales-2023-06-04.txt).
  The same source names number paths as a step before the line: "some teachers opt to introduce bead strings or
  number paths as transitional models".
- Errors: the sign that a child keeps the start apart from the steps. "Elongating the first counting word
  (“Niiiiine...”) is natural and indicates that the student differentiates between the first addend and the counts
  for the second addend." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 14).
- Errors: counting back. "Note: Many children attempt to count down for subtraction, but counting down is difficult
  and error-prone. Children are much more successful with counting on; it makes subtraction as easy as addition."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 36, Appendix).
- Errors: a line without 0. "Always include 0 on your number line. (Forgetting about 0 can get children into a real
  muddle!)" (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt, Blank
  number lines).
- Errors: counting on learnt as a routine. "Counting on should be seen as a thinking strategy, not a rote method."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 14).

## 040 difference

- What the reader must hold from `more-and-less`: matching two groups and saying which has more. "Compare problems
  build upon Kindergarten comparisons, in which students identified ”Which is more?” or “Which is less?” without
  ascertaining the difference between the numbers." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 12).
- Order: matching first, a bar diagram later. "Matching with objects and with drawings, and labeling each quantity
  (e.g., J or Julie and L or Lucy) is helpful. Later in Grade 1, a tape diagram can be used."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 12).
- Order: NCETM gives the difference last of the meanings of subtraction, in Year 2: "Introduce children to
  subtraction as difference, the third and final subtraction structure"
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-primm-112-subtraction-as-difference.txt, Introduction). IM teaches comparing
  within 10 in grade 1 unit 2, before unit 3 on numbers to 20: "Section C: Compare Story Problems"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2).
- Order: why the difference comes later. "One reason Compare problems are more advanced than the other two major
  types is that in Compare problems, one of the quantities (the difference) is not present in the situation
  physically" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 12).
- Order: subtraction answered by adding on. US grade 1: "Understand subtraction as an unknown-addend problem. For
  example, subtract 10 – 8 by finding the number that makes 10 when added to 8." (.cache/curricula/us-common-core-math.pdf,
  page 15, 1.OA.B.4). "Counting on for subtraction is easier than counting down."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 15).
- Examples: two ages. "John is 15 and William is 12, how much younger is William than John?" written "either: 12 + ?
  = 15 or 15 – 12 = ?", then "Starting at 12, children will count on 3 to reach 15"
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-jqmjvenm-pska-add-q3.txt, Subtraction as difference).
- Examples: unmatched objects. "“If there are 8 trucks and 5 drivers, how many trucks do not have a driver?”"
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 12).
- Examples: two towers of cubes, with counting either way. "They may start at 3 and count up to 10 or start at 10
  and count back to 3." (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt,
  Unit 2, Section C).
- Examples: 7 − 4 turned into 4 + ? = 7 and counted on a line. "we start with the number to be taken away (usually
  the smaller number) and then see how many we need to add on, or count on, to reach the bigger number."
  (cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt, Counting on).
- Examples: pencils and pens, and "Aisha used 7 coloured pencils for her artwork. Lucas used 4 How many more
  coloured pencils did Aisha use than Lucas?" (thenational.academy-es-maths-primary-ks1-units-calculating-within-20-lessons-calculate-the-difference.txt,
  Assessment exit quiz).
- Words: "difference", with Oak's keyword "Difference - The result of subtracting one number from another."
  (thenational.academy-es-maths-primary-ks1-units-calculating-within-20-lessons-calculate-the-difference.txt,
  Keywords). England Year 1: "distance between, difference between, more than and less than"
  (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, page 7). NCETM calls it a gap: "the difference being the ‘gap’ between the
  two values" (ncetm.org.uk-media-jqmjvenm-pska-add-q3.txt, Subtraction as difference).
- Errors: the smaller number written first. "knowing that the difference between 4 and 6 is 2, but recording this as
  4 - 6 = 2". The advice: "remind children that subtraction is not commutative so the whole (/ the larger number of
  those being compared) has to be written first in the expression."
  (thenational.academy-es-maths-primary-ks1-units-calculating-within-20-lessons-calculate-the-difference.txt, Common
  misconception).
- Errors: the word "more" read as adding. "In the question, ‘How many more?’, children may focus on the word ‘more’,
  believing they need to do an addition calculation instead of finding the difference." and "adding together the
  values in the calculation" (ncetm.org.uk-media-jqmjvenm-pska-add-q3.txt, Common errors). The advice is a picture of
  two bars: "Using this representation means children make fewer errors when deciding on which calculation to use".
- Errors: hearing only who has more. "Many students “hear” the part of the sentence about who has more, but do not
  initially hear the part about how many more" and "some children think that “less” means “more.”"
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 12).
- Errors: one written form for all. "Some textbooks represent all Compare problems with a subtraction equation, but
  that is not how many students think of the subtypes." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 12).

## 050 making-ten

- What the reader must hold from `number-bonds`: "These make-a-ten methods have three prerequisites reaching back to
  Kindergarten: a. knowing the partner that makes 10 for any number ... b. knowing all decompositions for any number
  below 10 ... and c. knowing all teen numbers as 10 + n" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, pages 15
  and 16). NCETM: "they will need to be fluent at partitioning numbers up to ten in different ways."
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 1, Overview of learning).
- Order: three numbers with a pair that makes ten, then splitting the second number. "This is then combined with
  partitioning to give a useful strategy for adding two numbers which bridge the tens boundary"
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 1). "Introduce the idea that we can
  transform a sum of two addends (e.g. 7 + 5) into a sum of three addends (e.g. 7 + 3 + 2) by partitioning one of the
  addends." (same file, page 15, step 5:1).
- Order: a story, then ten frames, then a line, then the written lines alone. "Begin by using a story with a
  practical representation. Then transfer the context to tens frames to help children see the opportunity to make
  ten." "You can also represent this strategy using a number line." "Children should build towards using just the
  symbolic notation." (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, pages 15 and 16, steps
  5:1, 5:4, 5:5).
- Order: adding across ten first, then subtracting, and two ways to subtract. "To subtract through ten, we subtract
  down to ten, and then subtract the rest of the subtrahend from ten, for example: 12 − 4 = 12 − 2 − 2 = 8" (step 6:1)
  and then "To subtract from ten, we subtract the subtrahend from ten, and then add on the difference between the
  minuend and ten, for example: 12 − 4 = (10 + 2) − 4 = (10 − 4) + 2 = 8" (step 6:2)
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, pages 19 and 20).
- Order: which way suits which numbers. "Subtracting through ten may be easier when the subtrahend is smaller (e.g.
  12 − 3) and subtracting from ten possibly lends itself to larger subtrahends (e.g. 17 − 9)."
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 23, step 6:5).
- Order: Oak gives down to ten for subtraction. "Partition the part being subtracted so that one of its smaller parts
  is equal to the ones digit. The remaining part can be subtracted from 10. Bridging through 10 can be represented on
  a number line." (thenational.academy-teachers-lessons-add-and-subtract-two-numbers-that-bridge-through-10.txt, Key
  learning points).
- Order: IM teaches adding across ten in Section C and subtracting in Section D, with both down to ten and counting
  on: "given \(15 - 8\), students may take away 5 to get to 10 and then take away another 3" and "They may also start
  with 8 and count on by 2 to get 10, and then add another 5 to reach 15."
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 3, Section D).
- Examples: a ride with ten seats in a carriage. "‘A ride at the funfair has ten seats in each carriage. You have to
  fill up the whole carriage before children can get in a new one. There are seven children in the first carriage.
  Five more get on.’" (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 15, step 5:1).
- Examples: the sums practised. "7+6 9+4 9+8 7+7 7+4 8+8 6+8 9+3 7+9 8+6 8+3 9+7 8+5 8+4 9+6 7+5 4+8 7+8 6+6 5+7
  5+8" (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 17, step 5:6). The subtractions:
  "11 − 3 = 14 − 6 = 12 − 9 = 16 − 7 =" (same file, page 24, step 6:7).
- Examples: 8 + 5 and 9 + 6 split. "Partition 5 in this equation to find the sum. 8 + 5 =" and "Andeep has the
  equation 9 + 6 = ___ How has he partitioned 6 to be able to bridge through 10?"
  (thenational.academy-teachers-lessons-add-and-subtract-two-numbers-that-bridge-through-10.txt, quizzes).
- Words: the sentence a child says. "‘First I partition the ___: ___ plus ____ is equal to ____’ ‘Then ___ plus ___
  is equal to ten…’ ‘…and ten plus ___ is equal to ___.’" (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt,
  page 16, step 5:2). For subtraction: "‘First we took away two counters to make ten. Then we took away another two
  counters to make eight.’" (same file, page 19, step 6:1).
- Words: Oak's keywords "Partition", "Whole", "Part" and "Bridging 10 - Addition or subtraction that crosses the 10
  boundary." (thenational.academy-teachers-lessons-add-and-subtract-two-numbers-that-bridge-through-10.txt, Keywords).
  NCETM: "a ‘make ten’ strategy" (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-primm-111-addition-and-subtraction-bridging-10.txt,
  Teaching point 5).
- Errors: a chain of equals. "Some children may write: 12 − 2 = 10 − 2 = 8 Make sure that you pick up this error, and
  show that 10 − 2 = 8 must be written as a separate equation to avoid differing values on either side of the equals
  symbol." (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, pages 19 and 20, step 6:1).
- Errors: counting over the frames. "Ensure that children use the tens frames to expose the mathematical structure
  and that they do not, for example, use them to ‘count all’ or ‘count on’ without making ten."
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 16, step 5:3). For subtraction: "without
  using counters and without counting back in ones to get an answer" (same file, page 23, step 6:6).
- Errors: dropping the frame too soon. "Children may make mistakes when adopting more mental strategies. Leave
  scaffolds in place until showing confidence in verbal explanation."
  (thenational.academy-teachers-lessons-add-and-subtract-two-numbers-that-bridge-through-10.txt, Common
  misconception).
- Errors: the English names of the teens. "The make-a-ten methods are more difficult in English than in East Asian
  languages in which teen numbers are spoken as ten, ten one, ten two, ten three, etc."
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 16).

## Against the brief

- Question 1, the order of the meanings of subtraction: NCETM gives the missing part first, then taking away, then the
  difference last: "Teaching point 4: Breaking a whole down into two or more parts is called partitioning; the
  subtraction symbol, −, can be used to represent partitioning." (topic 1.5, ncetm.org.uk-oom-resources-primm-105-additive-structures-introduction-to-aggregation-and-partitioning.txt),
  "subtraction as reduction (take away)" (topic 1.6, ncetm.org.uk-sroom-resources-primm-106-additive-structures-introduction-to-augmentation-and-reduction.txt),
  "the third and final subtraction structure" (topic 1.12, ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-primm-112-subtraction-as-difference.txt).
  Bremen lists "Wegnehmen, Ergänzen, Vergleichen" (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 21). The
  brief has taking away, then the difference, and gives the missing part to no section of this story.
- NCETM teaches the difference after bridging ten: topic "1.11 Addition and subtraction: bridging 10" comes before
  "1.12 Subtraction as difference", both in Year 2
  (ncetm.org.uk-stery-materials-primary-mastery-professional-development-number-addition-and-subtraction.txt). IM
  has the brief's order, comparing within 10 in unit 2 before making ten in unit 3
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt).
- Question 2, the signs: the sources join groups and take away without signs first, then give + and = together, and
  − with them. NCETM topic 1.5 follows the topics on parts and wholes: "Progress to the use of abstract notation
  (+, − and =)" (ncetm.org.uk-oom-resources-primm-105-additive-structures-introduction-to-aggregation-and-partitioning.txt).
  This fits the brief if the opening page joins two groups before the page of the sign +.
- The brief makes "sum" the word of section 010. England gives "add, altogether, total" in Year 1 and "sum" in
  Year 2 (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf, pages 7 and 12), the progression advises "it is better in the earlier
  grades to use “total” rather than “sum.”" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 8), and Bremen
  gives "Summe" by year 4 (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 39). NCETM uses "whole/sum" in
  Year 1 (ncetm.org.uk-oom-resources-primm-105-additive-structures-introduction-to-aggregation-and-partitioning.txt).
- The words for the sign −: the brief says "the sign − stands between the number at the start and the number taken
  away". NCETM warns "it is important that the subtraction sign is not referred to as ‘take away’"
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-pkxnnz0r-pska-add-q2.txt), and Bremen and Oxford give "minus"
  (.cache/curricula/germany-bremen-primary-2025.pdf, page 39,
  cdn.oxfordowl.co.uk-2014-05-13-10-29-46-467-PX-MathsContent-BK-SubtractionInSchool-01-CH.txt). The sign needs a
  name that also fits the difference in section 040.
- Question 3, the start of counting on: the brief counts on "from the first number". The progression counts on "from
  the first addend (or the larger number is taken as the first addend)"
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 37), and Eureka Math moves to the larger number only after
  the order of the numbers is free (onlinemathlearning.com-count-on-larger-addend.txt, Lesson 20). The story
  `any-order` follows this story, so the first number fits the brief.
- Question 3, the errors: counting the start as a step (nrich.maths.org-5652.txt, "counting the jumps they make, and
  not the starting point"), counting the marks (oise.utoronto.ca-robertson-blog-power-number-line-model-scales-2023-06-04.txt,
  "lots of students just count the hash marks"), and counting back: "counting down is difficult and error-prone"
  (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 36). The brief teaches counting back as the way to subtract
  in section 030, and the progression prefers counting on for subtraction, which the brief has in section 040.
- The number line for a first reader: "young children have difficulties with the number line representation because
  they have difficulty seeing the units" and "some teachers opt to introduce bead strings or number paths as
  transitional models" (oise.utoronto.ca-robertson-blog-power-number-line-model-scales-2023-06-04.txt). The brief
  draws jumps on a line from 0 to 20, so each jump has to show as an arc from one number to the next.
- The difference as a subtraction: the brief writes it as the greater minus the smaller, as Oak does: "the whole (/ the larger
  number of those being compared) has to be written first in the expression." (thenational.academy-es-maths-primary-ks1-units-calculating-within-20-lessons-calculate-the-difference.txt).
  The progression adds "Some textbooks represent all Compare problems with a subtraction equation, but that is not
  how many students think of the subtypes." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 12), and NCETM
  writes both "12 + ? = 15 or 15 – 12 = ?" (ncetm.org.uk-media-jqmjvenm-pska-add-q3.txt).
- Question 4, making ten for subtraction: down to ten is the first way in NCETM, Oak, IM and the US standard: "To
  subtract through ten, we subtract down to ten" (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt,
  page 19), "decomposing a number leading to a ten (e.g., 13 – 4 = 13 – 3 – 1 = 10 – 1 = 9)"
  (.cache/curricula/us-common-core-math.pdf, page 15). NCETM then teaches taking from the ten as a second way, "Teaching
  point 6: We can subtract across the tens boundary by subtracting through ten or subtracting from ten."
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-primm-111-addition-and-subtraction-bridging-10.txt), and the progression says
  down to ten "can be more difficult than the forward methods" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt,
  page 15). The brief has only down to ten.
- Question 5, sums such as 12 + 5: IM teaches them in grade 1 before making ten, "Here students work only with
  expressions that do not require composing or decomposing a ten, for example, \(13-2\) and \(12+5\)"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 3, Section B).
  England Year 1 has "add and subtract one-digit and two-digit numbers to 20" (.cache/curricula/england-ks1-2.pdf,
  page 7), and Japan grade 1 has "simple cases of sums and differences involving 2-digit numbers"
  (.cache/curricula/japan-course-of-study-2008-en.pdf, page 7). The progression puts them with place value: "Other
  Grade 1 problems within 20, such as 14 + 5, are best viewed in the context of place value, i.e., associated with
  1.NBT.4." (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 12, the file drops the sign), and NCETM teaches them
  in Year 2 topic 1.13 (ncetm.org.uk-lassroom-resources-primm-113-addition-and-subtraction-two-digit-and-single-digit-numbers.txt).
  IM, England and Japan teach them with this subject, and the progression and NCETM put them with place value. The
  brief has none of them, and `tens-and-ones`, the story of the teen numbers, is not among its Needs.
- Making ten needs every split of the numbers below 10, besides the pairs that make 10: "b. knowing all
  decompositions for any number below 10" (isbe.net-Documents-counting-cardinality-k-5.txt, page 15). The brief's
  Needs for section 050 name only the pairs that make 10.
- The sum grows by one and adding 0 are in the brief's section 010. NCETM teaches them with the strategies within 10
  in topic 1.7, after the signs in 1.5 and 1.6
  (ncetm.org.uk-classroom-resources-primm-107-addition-and-subtraction-strategies-within-10.txt).
- The numbers: NCETM keeps three part sums within ten before bridging ten: "The learning sequence begins with three
  addends which sum to ten or less, before exploring bridging ten."
  (ncetm.org.uk-media-x51ltghh-ncetm-mm-sp1-y2-se11-teach-final-ys2.txt, page 1). IM keeps the first comparing and
  missing parts within 10: "The numbers are kept within 10 so students can focus on interpreting each problem"
  (curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org-k5-teachers-grade-1-course-guide-scope-and-sequence.txt, Unit 2). The brief
  uses 12 − 5 and 15 − 4 for the first take away.
